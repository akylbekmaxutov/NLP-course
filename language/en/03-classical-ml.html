Theory · Why bother

Why classical algorithms, in the age of LLMs?

Because for a great many real tasks they are still the right answer — and because everything that comes later is easier to understand once you have seen a model learn from counted words.

They need little data

Eight thousand reviews and a laptop are enough to reach 0.784 on this task. For most languages other than English, datasets of that size are what you actually have.

They are readable

You can print the exact words behind every decision. Try explaining a refused loan application with “the neural network said so”.

They are cheap and fast

Training takes seconds on a laptop, prediction takes microseconds, and deployment is a file of numbers — no GPU.

They are the baseline

You cannot claim a large model helps until a TF-IDF baseline has been beaten. Surprisingly often, it has not been.

The honest limit. These models see only the words you counted. Word order, meaning and context are still out of reach — everything Lecture 2 warned about. When the task needs those, the later lectures are where you go.
Theory · The map

The supervised learning pipeline

Supervised learning means learning from examples that already carry the right answer. You give the model pairs of (text, label); it finds the pattern that connects them.

  • Labelled text→
  • TF-IDF features→
  • Train / test split→
  • Model→
  • Metrics→
  • Error analysis

The last arrow points back to the first box. Error analysis tells you what to change — usually the data, not the algorithm — and you go round again. That loop is the actual job.

Theory · The data

The dataset: KazSAnDRA

KazSAnDRA — the Kazakh Sentiment Analysis Dataset of Reviews and Attitudes — collects reviews from app stores, marketplaces, maps and bookstores. We use its polarity split: every review is either negative (0) or positive (1).

The dataset is gated. Accept the terms at huggingface.co/datasets/issai/kazsandra and put HF_TOKEN=hf_… in a .env file. python-dotenv reads it, datasets downloads and caches the split, and to_pandas() hands it to pandas.

With 82% positive, accuracy stops meaning anything. We take an equal number of each class for training and for testing, so 0.500 is the score of a coin flip and every point above it is real learning.

Algorithm 1

Naive Bayes

Naive Bayes asks a simple question: which class was most likely to have produced these words? It multiplies the probability of each word given each class, and picks the winner.

The rule behind it is Bayes' theorem, with one bold simplification:

P(class | words) ∝ P(class) × P(word₁ | class) × P(word₂ | class) × …

The simplification — the “naive” part — is that the words are assumed independent of each other. That is plainly false in language: жақсы and емес are not independent. The model works anyway, which is one of the pleasant surprises of the field.

Two details that make it work

Logarithms

Multiplying hundreds of small probabilities underflows to zero, so we add logarithms instead. That is why the scores below are negative numbers.

Smoothing

A word never seen with a class would give probability 0 and wipe out the whole product. Laplace smoothing adds alpha to every count so nothing is ever impossible.

The whole model, in six lines

A Pipeline chains the vectorizer and the classifier into one object. That matters: the same vocabulary and the same weighting are applied at training and at prediction time, which is where hand-written pipelines usually go wrong. 

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline

model = Pipeline([
    ("tfidf", TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2, sublinear_tf=True)),
    ("clf", MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0)),
])
model.fit(x_train, y_train)

Three choices are worth naming. ngram_range=(1, 2) keeps single words and pairs, so өте нашар becomes its own feature. min_df=2 drops everything that appears in one review only — on this data that is most of the vocabulary. alpha=1.0 is the Laplace smoothing from the formula above.

Algorithm 2

Logistic Regression

Logistic Regression gives every word a weight, adds up the weights of the words present, and squeezes the total into a probability between 0 and 1. Training means adjusting the weights until the predictions match the labels. 

z = w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + … + b        P(class) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)
  • positive
  • negative

Every document is a point; the model looks for a straight line that separates the classes. In reality the space has one dimension per word — thousands of them — but the picture is the same.

Compared with Naive Bayes it drops the independence assumption: weights are fitted together, so a word that only matters next to another one can be down-weighted. It usually wins on larger datasets, and it gives calibrated probabilities you can threshold.

The one parameter you will actually tune. C controls regularisation: small C keeps the weights small and the model simple, large C lets it fit the training data closely — and overfit. Start at C=1 and move by factors of ten.
Algorithm 3

Support Vector Machines

An SVM also draws a line — but not just any line. It looks for the one with the widest empty corridor around it, the margin. Only the points on the edge of that corridor matter; they are the support vectors.

The shaded corridor is the margin. The two outlined points are the support vectors — move any other point and the boundary does not change at all.

For text this matters: with thousands of sparse features, many separating lines fit the training data perfectly, and the widest-margin one generalises best. LinearSVC is often the strongest classical baseline for text classification.

Linear kernel, always — for text. The RBF kernel is famous, but on high-dimensional sparse text data it is slower and rarely better. Use LinearSVC. Its one drawback: it gives no probabilities, only decisions — if you need a confidence score, use Logistic Regression.
Algorithms 4 and 5

k Nearest Neighbours, trees and forests

kNN — no training at all

kNN stores the training set and, for a new document, finds the k most similar ones — cosine similarity from Lecture 2 — and takes a vote.

The cross is a new review. Inside the circle: two positive neighbours and one negative, so the vote says positive.

The cost moves. There is nothing to train, but every prediction compares the new review against all 8 000 training reviews. On this dataset that makes kNN far slower than Naive Bayes at prediction time, for no gain in accuracy — the reason it is rarely used for text.

Decision trees and random forests

A decision tree asks yes/no questions — “does the review contain нашар?” — and follows the branches to a class. A single tree overfits badly; a random forest trains hundreds of them on random subsets and averages the votes.

  • contains “нашар”?→
  • yes → negative
  • no → contains “керемет”?→
  • yes → positive
And this is what they actually score here. The single tree is the weakest model in the lecture — 0.750 on the test set, 0.732 under cross-validation, with double the fold-to-fold variance of the linear models. The forest repairs most of that (0.775) but still does not catch Naive Bayes, while fitting almost perfectly on the training data (0.980) and taking five times longer.
Why they struggle. A tree splits on one feature at a time, and a TF-IDF matrix has 9 419 sparse columns where almost every value is zero. Use trees for text when you combine words with other features — review length, rating history, time of day — because mixing feature types is exactly what they are good at.
Theory · Choosing

Which algorithm, when

Comparison of the classical algorithms
Algorithm Strength Weakness Reach for it when…
Naive Bayes trains instantly, works on tiny data assumes words are independent you need a baseline in five minutes
Logistic Regression calibrated probabilities, readable weights needs more data than Naive Bayes you need a confidence score or a threshold
Linear SVM strong on long documents and large vocabularies no probabilities documents are long and there is plenty of data
kNN no training, adapts as data arrives slow and weak at prediction time the dataset is small and changes constantly
Decision Tree the only model you can read as a flowchart weakest and least stable here: 0.750, ±0.012 you must explain a decision step by step
Random Forest mixes text with numeric features, 0.775 widest overfitting gap (+0.205), slowest to fit you have metadata as well as words
And then measure, because the table is only a prior. On this dataset the ranking is the reverse of the usual advice: Naive Bayes 0.784, Logistic Regression 0.780, Random Forest 0.775, Linear SVM 0.762, Decision Tree 0.750. Short reviews and a small vocabulary favour the simplest model. Running all five costs about ten seconds — let the numbers decide.
Theory · Measuring

Metrics: accuracy is not enough

Precision

Of everything the model called negative, how much really was? Punishes false alarms.

Recall

Of all the real negative reviews, how many did it find? Punishes misses.

F1

The harmonic mean of the two — one number when you must have one number.

Confusion matrix

Which class is mistaken for which. The only one that tells you what to fix.

From the run above: negative precision 0.782, recall 0.787. The two are nearly equal, which means the model is not trading one for the other — it simply misses about a fifth of the complaints and raises about as many false alarms. Whether that is acceptable depends on which mistake costs more.

What happens without balancing

This is the same task, trained on the raw distribution instead:

train_raw = load("train")

    predicted = model.predict(x_test)
    recall_negative = ((predicted == 0) & (y_test == 0)).sum() / (y_test == 0).sum()

          f"  recall(negative)={recall_negative:.3f}")

Negative recall collapses from 0.787 to 0.154. Trained on 82% positive data, the model learns that answering “positive” is usually right and stops finding complaints almost entirely. And here is the trap: measured on the unbalanced test set that same model scores 0.842 — while a model that always answers “positive” scores 0.820. It looks respectable and it has learned almost nothing.
Theory · Trusting the number

How much data, and how much trust

Two questions worth asking about any reported accuracy: would more data improve it, and how much would it move if you had split differently?

More data

from lesson3 import load, balance, build

train_raw = load("train")
test = balance(load("test"), 1000)
x_test, y_test = test["text_cleaned"].astype(str), test["label"]

for per_class in (250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000):
    train = balance(train_raw, per_class)
    model = build("nb")
    model.fit(train["text_cleaned"].astype(str), train["label"])
    print(f"{2 * per_class:>5} training reviews → accuracy {model.score(x_test, y_test):.3f}")
  500 training reviews → accuracy 0.733
 1000 training reviews → accuracy 0.746
 2000 training reviews → accuracy 0.773
 4000 training reviews → accuracy 0.779
 8000 training reviews → accuracy 0.784

Sixteen times more data buys five points, and the curve is flattening. That is the shape to expect from a bag-of-words model: it climbs quickly, then stalls, because it never learns that two different word forms mean the same thing. Breaking through that ceiling needs different features, not more rows.

Cross-validation

A single test score is one sample. Five-fold cross-validation splits the training data five ways and trains five times, for every model: 

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from lesson3 import load, balance, build

train = balance(load("train"), 4000)
x, y = train["text_cleaned"].astype(str), train["label"]

for name in ("nb", "logreg", "svm", "tree", "forest"):
    scores = cross_val_score(build(name), x, y, cv=5)
    print(f"{name:>7}  {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}   {scores.round(3)}")
     nb  0.792 ± 0.006   [0.789 0.788 0.785 0.797 0.801]
 logreg  0.780 ± 0.006   [0.778 0.77  0.782 0.786 0.784]
    svm  0.768 ± 0.005   [0.768 0.761 0.768 0.772 0.774]
   tree  0.732 ± 0.012   [0.749 0.716 0.732 0.721 0.742]

The order is stable across folds, and the standard deviations separate the two families: 0.005–0.006 for the linear models against 0.011–0.012 for the trees. A single decision tree is both the weakest and the least stable. Naive Bayes leads Logistic Regression by 0.012, which is twice its own fold-to-fold spread — that is what makes the ranking believable.

Capacity and overfitting

Every model, this time on the training set against the test set, with the time each one took to fit: 

import time
from lesson3 import load, balance, build

train = balance(load("train"), 4000)
test = balance(load("test"), 1000)

    model = build(name)
    started = time.time()
    model.fit(x_train, y_train)
    seconds = time.time() - started
    train_score = model.score(x_train, y_train)
    test_score = model.score(x_test, y_test)
    print(f"{name:>7}  train={train_score:.3f}  test={test_score:.3f}"
          f"  gap={train_score - test_score:+.3f}  fit={seconds:5.1f}s")
     nb  train=0.867  test=0.784  gap=+0.083  fit=  0.1s
 logreg  train=0.923  test=0.780  gap=+0.142  fit=  0.1s
    svm  train=0.945  test=0.762  gap=+0.182  fit=  0.1s

The gap grows with the power of the model. Naive Bayes fits the training data least well (0.867) and generalises best (0.784). The random forest fits it almost perfectly (0.980) and carries the widest gap of all (+0.205). On 8 000 short reviews there is not enough signal to justify a flexible model — the extra capacity goes into memorising. Note the fit times too: the forest costs five times the linear models for a result that is still behind them.

Theory · A common trap

Class imbalance

Real data is never balanced. KazSAnDRA is 82% positive; a fraud dataset might be 99.9% clean. The rare class is usually the one you care about.

Weight the classes

class_weight="balanced" in scikit-learn makes each mistake on a rare class count more. One parameter, usually the first thing to try.

Resample

Drop examples of the frequent class, or duplicate the rare one. Cheap, and it throws away information or invites overfitting — respectively.

Move the threshold

With Logistic Regression you decide the cut-off yourself: predict “fraud” at probability 0.2 rather than 0.5 when a miss is expensive.

Report the right metric

Use macro-F1 or per-class recall. Accuracy on imbalanced data is a number that makes everyone comfortable and tells no one anything.

Theory · Looking inside

What the model learned

Every model here can be asked which features push hardest towards each class. On real reviews this is the fastest way to find out whether it learned sentiment or something else.

print("negative:", ", ".join(negative))
print("positive:", ", ".join(positive))
negative: нашар, не, ұнамады, өте жаман, қате, мүлдем, түсініксіз, шығып, жаман, өте нашар
positive: жақсы, рахмет, керемет, keremet, ыңғайлы, жаман емес, жаксы, мықты, ұнайды, топ

Both lists are sentiment, which is what you want to see: нашар (bad), ұнамады (did not like), түсініксіз (unclear) against жақсы (good), рахмет (thanks), ыңғайлы (convenient). Look at the bigrams. өте нашар (very bad) and маған ұнамады carry more than their words alone — and жаман емес, “not bad”, sits in the positive list. A unigram model sees only жаман there and gets it backwards. That is the negation problem from Lecture 2, solved by ngram_range=(1, 2).

Real review data also contains profanity, and it lands high in the negative list because it is genuinely predictive. lesson3.py skips two such tokens when printing. A production system needs a decision about them, not an accident.

The mistakes

true=positive predicted=negative confidence=0.52  «Мен қазақшамың қазақстанда тұрамын»
true=positive predicted=negative confidence=0.62  «Ма шааЛлагь»
true=negative predicted=positive confidence=0.56  «Бауырларым менде жүмғой»
true=positive predicted=negative confidence=0.68  «Жаңа әндер мен өлеңдер жинағы мен сені чаттан»

Read the reviews, not just the labels. «Мен қазақшамың қазақстанда тұрамын» — “I am Kazakh-speaking, I live in Kazakhstan” — carries no sentiment at all, yet it is labelled positive. «Ма шааЛлагь» is an exclamation. These are not model failures; they are reviews that contain no opinion, and no classifier can recover a signal that is not there. Note the confidence column: 0.52 and 0.56 mean the model was almost undecided, which is exactly what a review with no sentiment should produce.

Kazakhstan

What makes Kazakh reviews hard

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from lesson3 import load, balance

train = balance(load("train"), 4000)

frequency = counts.sum(axis=0).A1
vectorizer = CountVectorizer().fit(train["text_cleaned"].astype(str))

print("reviews          :", counts.shape[0])
print("vocabulary       :", counts.shape[1])
print("tokens           :", int(frequency.sum()))

      f"({100 * (frequency == 1).mean():.1f}% of the vocabulary)")
reviews          : 8000
vocabulary       : 19125
tokens           : 71478
seen exactly once: 13266 (69.4% of the vocabulary)

69% of the vocabulary appears exactly once. Nineteen thousand distinct forms from seventy-two thousand tokens — the model sees most words a single time and can learn almost nothing from them. This is the morphology lesson from Lecture 2 arriving as a number: every case and possessive suffix creates another form that Bag of Words treats as an unrelated word.

Three problems, all visible in the data
  • Morphology splits the evidence. The frequent words are the ones that resist inflection — өте (very), жақсы (good), керемет (great). Anything inflected is scattered across forms.
  • Reviews are very short. A median of six words, 23% with three or fewer. «Әлі қатады го» carries almost no signal for a word-counting model.
  • The text is mixed. Russian words (регистрация, реклама, ужас) sit inside Kazakh sentences, exactly the code-switching from Lecture 0 — and they are among the most predictive features.
What to try next. Character n-grams — TfidfVectorizer(analyzer="char_wb", ngram_range=(3, 5)) — catch shared stems across inflected forms and usually beat word features on Kazakh. Lemmatization would do the same job if a good analyser is available. Both are exercises at the end of this page.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

    model.fit(x_train, y_train)
    print(name, model.score(x_test, y_test))

In class

Knowledge check

Five questions to answer together before we finish.

Question 1

KazSAnDRA is 82% positive. Why did we balance it, and what would accuracy mean if we had not?

Show Answer

On the raw distribution a model that always answers “positive” scores 0.82, so accuracy no longer separates a working model from a useless one. After balancing, 0.500 is the coin flip and 0.784 is real. We measured the cost of not balancing: negative recall falls from 0.787 to 0.154.

Question 2

What exactly is "naive" about Naive Bayes, and why does it still work here?

Show Answer

It assumes words are independent given the class, which is false for language. For classification only the ranking matters — the right class needs the highest score, and the probabilities themselves can be badly wrong.

Question 3

Negative precision is 0.825 but recall is 0.735. Explain the difference to a product manager.

Show Answer

When the system flags a review as a complaint, it is right about 83% of the time. But it only catches 74% of the real complaints — one in four is missed. Which number matters depends on whether a false alarm or a missed complaint costs more.

Question 4

Cross-validation gives 0.792 ± 0.006, the single test split gives 0.784. Which do you report?

Show Answer

Roughly 0.78, and say which set it came from. Cross-validation uses only training data, the test split is held out, and the small difference between them is well inside the fold-to-fold spread. Never report the higher of two numbers without saying how it was measured.

Question 5

«Жақсы бəрін көруге болады» is labelled negative; the model says positive. Who is wrong?

Show Answer

Probably the label. Real datasets contain annotation noise, and part of every accuracy gap belongs to the data rather than the model. Before tuning anything, read a sample of the errors and estimate how many are actually label problems.

Work in the folder with lesson3.py — every task is a few lines on top of it.

Task 1 — Run all three

Run lesson3.py with --train-size 250, 1000 and 4000. Plot accuracy against training size. Where does the curve flatten?

Show Answer

0.727 → 0.752 → 0.778, still rising but slowly. Sixteen times the data buys about six points, so the next gain has to come from better features, not more rows.

Task 2 — Break smoothing

Change MultinomialNB(alpha=…) to 0.01, 0.1, 1 and 5. Which wins, and why do both extremes lose?

Show Answer

Small alpha trusts rare words too much; large alpha flattens the evidence towards the class prior. Report the four numbers you measure, and tune on validation data — never on the test set you report.

Task 3 — Domain shift

Train on one domain only (appstore) and test on another (market). How far does accuracy fall?

Show Answer

Expect a drop: app-store complaints and marketplace complaints use different vocabulary. This is domain shift, and it is why a model validated on one source can fail on another.

Task 4 — Character n-grams

Change tokenize() to produce character 4-grams instead of words. Does accuracy improve?

Show Answer

Character n-grams usually help on Kazakh, because they share pieces between inflected forms that word features treat as unrelated. Report the number you actually measure, not the one you expect.

Task 5 — Imbalance

Train on 4000 positive and only 400 negative reviews. Report accuracy and per-class recall.

Show Answer

Accuracy stays respectable while negative recall collapses — the same effect as the raw-distribution experiment, only sharper.

Task 6 — Leaky split

Copy 200 test reviews into the training set and rerun. What happens to accuracy, and what is this mistake called?

Show Answer

Accuracy rises for free — the model is tested on what it memorised. This is data leakage. Duplicate reviews are common in scraped data, so it happens by accident more often than by mistake.

Task 7 — Error analysis

Sort the test set by the gap between the two class scores. Read the ten smallest gaps.

Show Answer

Use score() from lesson3.py. The smallest gaps are the reviews that would flip on a slightly different training set — and usually the most interesting ones to read.

Task 8 — Real data

Set min_df to 1, 2 and 5 in the TfidfVectorizer. How does the feature count change, and does accuracy follow?

Show Answer

min_df=2 already removes most of the vocabulary, because 69% of the words appear once. Fewer features usually means a smaller gap between train and test. Compare any gain against the fold spread of about 0.006 before claiming it.

Task 9 — Build a Kazakh dataset

Read 50 negative reviews from the dataset. How many would you have labelled differently, and what rule would settle those cases?

Show Answer

Expect to disagree with a few. Written rules matter more than the data itself: without them two annotators disagree and the model learns the disagreement. Projects measure this as inter-annotator agreement.

Task 10 — Beat the baseline

Beat 0.784 on the same balanced test set. Anything is allowed except touching the test set.

Show Answer

Character n-grams, bigrams, more training data and a linear SVM are the candidates. Verify any gain against the fold spread before claiming it.

Self-check quiz

Ten questions with instant feedback.

  1. What does "supervised" mean?

    The label is the supervision — the model learns from (text, answer) pairs.

  2. Why does Naive Bayes add logarithms instead of multiplying probabilities?

    Adding logs is the same ranking, without the numerical collapse.

  3. What is Laplace smoothing for?

    One zero would wipe out the entire product — alpha keeps everything possible.

  4. A model scores 0.98 on training data and 0.78 on the test set. What is this?

    The model fits the training set better than reality — the gap is the symptom.

  5. The model finds 735 of the 1000 real negative reviews. Which metric is 0.735?

    Recall counts how many real cases were found: 735 of 1000.

  6. Why balance the test set as well as the training set?

    On the raw 82% positive test set the same model reports about 0.85 and looks better than it is.

  7. Which model gives you a probability you can threshold?

    The sigmoid outputs a calibrated probability; LinearSVC returns only a decision.

  8. 69% of the vocabulary appears exactly once. What does that cause?

    Kazakh morphology spreads one meaning across many forms, and a form seen once teaches nothing.

  9. On this dataset, which model generalised best?

    0.784 against 0.780 and 0.762 — on short reviews the simplest model won, and cross-validation confirmed the order.

  10. Trained on the raw 82% positive data, negative recall drops to 0.154. Why?

    The class prior does the work: with 82% positive, answering “positive” is cheap and mostly right.
