Motivation

Before models understand language, we first need to work with text.

Every NLP pipeline starts with plain, messy text: extra spaces, phone numbers, dates, hashtags, mixed languages. The oldest and still the most used tool for that first pass is:

Regular Expressions — Regex

Definition

What are Regular Expressions?

Regular Expressions are patterns used to find, extract, validate, split, or replace text.

The engine slides along the text and retries the pattern at every position. A match is nothing more than a starting point and a length.

  • Text +
  • Pattern =
  • Match

A Regex does not understand meaning. It only describes shape. And every pattern is built from just four kinds of piece — which is exactly how we will go through them:

1

What character?

Character classes: \d, \w, [абв]

2

How many times?

Quantifiers: +, *, ?, {n}

3

This or that?

Alternation and groups: |, ( )

4

Where exactly?

Anchors: ^, $, \b

Building block 1

Character classes — what character?

A character class matches one character. This is the smallest piece of every pattern.

A class is a menu, and the engine picks exactly one item from it per match. Two vowels in «салем» means two separate one-character matches.

Character classes with examples
Pattern Matches one… Example
\d digit 0-9 \d\d\d → 701
\D character that is not a digit \D → the space in +7 701
\w word character (letter, digit, _) #\w+ → #Astana
\s whitespace: space, tab, newline \s → the gap between words
\S anything that is not whitespace \S+ → one whole word
. any character at all А.тана → Астана
[абв] one of the listed characters [АӘ] → А or Ә
[^абв] anything except those [^0-9] → any non-digit
[а-я] a range of characters [A-Z] → K in KZK
Kazakh letters are a range too

The Kazakh alphabet adds letters that plain [а-я] does not cover. Write them in explicitly:

[а-яёәғқңөұүһі]

Capital letters: [А-ЯЁӘҒҚҢӨҰҮҺІ]

Careful with \w. In JavaScript it means only [A-Za-z0-9_] — Cyrillic letters are not included. In Python 3 it is Unicode-aware and does match Астана. We come back to this in the section on anchors.
Building block 2

Quantifiers — how many times?

A quantifier repeats the piece written directly before it.

Blue cells are required, grey ones optional. Adding ? after a quantifier makes it lazy: it stops at the first chance instead of the last.

Quantifiers with examples
Pattern Means Example
+ one or more \d+ → 2026
* zero or more \s* → matches even if there is no space
? zero or one — optional https? → http and https
{4} exactly four times \d{4} → 1991
{2,4} from two to four times \d{2,4} → 12, 123, 1234
{3,} three or more times \d{3,} → 7172

The optional ? in practice

One pattern, two spellings 
Сайт http://egov.kz және https://astana.kz ашық.

Pattern: https?://\S+

Result:

  • http://egov.kz
  • https://astana.kz

The ? applies only to the s right before it — “an s may or may not be here”.

Piece by piece
http
these four letters, exactly as written
s?
an optional s — ? means “zero or one”, and it applies only to the single character in front of it
://
three more literal characters
\S+
one or more characters that are not whitespace — the rest of the address

In words: http, maybe an s, then ://, then everything up to the next space.

Detail: \S+ stops only at a space, so in Сайт https://astana.kz. Келесі… it also swallows the final dot and returns https://astana.kz. — a good reminder that “until the next space” is not the same as “until the end of the link”.

Greedy by default

+ and * take as much text as they possibly can. Adding ? after them makes them lazy — they stop at the first opportunity.

Greedy vs lazy 
<b>Астана</b> және <b>Алматы</b>

<.+> — greedy, one huge match:

  • <b>Астана</b> және <b>Алматы</b>

<.+?> — lazy, four small matches:

  • <b>
  • </b>
  • <b>
  • </b>

This single character is one of the most common sources of “my Regex matches too much”.

Piece by piece
<
a literal left angle bracket
.+
any characters, one or more — . is “anything”, + is “at least one”
>
a literal right angle bracket
.+?
the same, but lazy: the ? after + tells it to stop as early as it can

In words: find a <, then some characters, then a > — and the only difference is how far “some characters” reaches.

Why it happens: a greedy + first runs to the very end of the text, then walks backwards until the rest of the pattern fits. The last > in the line fits, so it keeps everything. The lazy version starts short and grows only when it has to, so it settles on the first >.

Building block 3

Alternation and groups — this or that?

| has the lowest precedence in the whole language, so it splits everything around it. Brackets are how you tell it where to stop.

Alternation: | means OR

Any of several cities 
Астана, Алматы және Шымкент — үлкен қалалар.

Pattern: Астана|Алматы|Шымкент

Result:

  • Астана
  • Алматы
  • Шымкент
Piece by piece
Астана
the first alternative
|
OR — the engine tries the alternatives left to right and takes the first one that fits at that position
Алматы
the second alternative
Шымкент
the third alternative

In words: at this position, match Астана, or Алматы, or Шымкент.

Watch the scope: | splits the whole pattern, not just the word next to it. ^Астана|Алматы means “Астана at the start” OR “Алматы anywhere”, so in Астанада Алматы бар it finds both. Wrap the alternatives in brackets — ^(?:Астана|Алматы) — and only Астана at the start matches.

Groups: ( ) keep things together

Brackets do two jobs at once: they decide what a quantifier or an | applies to, and they capture the matched piece so you can read it back.

Capturing the parts of a date 
Кездесу 24.08.2026 күні өтеді.

Pattern: (\d{2})\.(\d{2})\.(\d{4})

whole match
24.08.2026
group 1 — day
24
group 2 — month
08
group 3 — year
2026

Groups turn “find a date” into “find a date and take it apart”.

Piece by piece
(
opens group 1 — groups are numbered by the order of their opening brackets
\d{2}
exactly two digits — the day
)
closes group 1
\.
an escaped dot: a real dot character, not “any character”
(\d{2})
group 2 — the month
(\d{4})
group 3 — the year

In words: two digits, a dot, two digits, a dot, four digits — and remember each number block separately.

Detail: the whole match is always available as group 0, and the captured pieces start at 1. In Python that is m.group(0), m.group(1), … — but careful: re.findall returns the groups instead of the whole match as soon as the pattern has any, which is why the next example uses non-capturing brackets.

Grouping without capturing

(?:…) groups without keeping the result — useful when you only need the brackets for structure: 

Билет 1 500 теңге, кофе 990 тг, тағы бірі 12 000 теңге.

Pattern: \d+(?:\s\d{3})*\s*(?:теңге|тг)

Result:

  • 1 500 теңге
  • 990 тг
  • 12 000 теңге
Piece by piece
\d+
one or more digits — matches 990, or the 1 of 1 500
(?:
opens a group that is not captured — brackets used only for structure
\s\d{3}
a space followed by exactly three digits — one thousands block, like  500
)*
zero or more of those blocks: none for 990, one for 1 500, one for 12 000
\s*
an optional space before the currency word
(?:теңге|тг)
either spelling of the currency, again without capturing

In words: a number, possibly written with spaces between thousands, followed by теңге or тг.

Why (?:…) and not (…): with plain brackets, re.findall would return only what the brackets caught — ['теңге', 'тг'] — instead of the whole price. Non-capturing brackets give the grouping without changing what you get back.

Building block 4

Anchors and boundaries — where exactly?

Anchors match a position, not a character. They consume nothing; they only say “here”.

The blue and red bars sit between the letters, not on them. That is what “zero-width” means — an anchor never uses up a character.

^ start of the text

^Астана matches only if the text begins with that word.

$ end of the text

\.$ matches a final full stop.

\b word boundary

\b\d{4}\b matches 2017 as a standalone number, not four digits inside a longer one.

\B not a boundary

The opposite of \b — useful for finding something inside a word.

A trap worth remembering. In JavaScript \b is defined through \w, which is ASCII only. So \bАстана\b matches nothing in a browser, while the same pattern works in Python. With Kazakh or Russian text, either drop the \b, or switch on the u flag and use \p{L} for “any letter”.
Unicode-safe patterns 
Бүгін #Astana және #Астана туралы жаздық.
#\w+
#Astana — the Cyrillic hashtag is missed
#[^\s#]+
#Astana, #Астана — works everywhere
#[\p{L}\d_]+ with flag u
#Astana, #Астана — the precise way

Try all three in the playground below: the Unicode (u) switch turns on \p{L}.

Building block 5

Escaping — when a character means itself

These characters have a special job inside a pattern:

Without the backslash, . matches any character at all — so the pattern would also accept text that is not a domain.

. + * ? ( ) [ ] { } | ^ $ \

To match one of them literally, put a backslash in front of it.

Escaped characters
Wrong Right Why
\d{2}.\d{2} \d{2}\.\d{2} a bare . matches any character, so 24x08 would pass
+7 \+7 a bare + means “one or more” and needs something before it
(2026) \(2026\) brackets create a group instead of matching brackets
Inside [ ] most characters lose their special meaning, which is why [\w.-] needs no escaping for the dot. In Python, re.escape("+7 (701)") escapes a string for you.

Putting it together

Regex with Kazakhstan data

Every pattern below is only the building blocks from above, put next to each other.

Example 01 · Phone numbers

Kazakhstan phone numbers

Менің нөмірім +7 701 123 45 67.
Кеңсе: +7 7172 55 44 33.

Pattern: \+7[\s\d]+

  • +7 701 123 45 67
  • +7 7172 55 44 33
Piece by piece
\+
an escaped plus — a bare + would mean “one or more” and would need something in front of it
7
the digit 7, literally
[\s\d]
one character that is either whitespace or a digit
+
one or more of those characters

In words: a literal +7, then any run of digits and spaces.

Where it stops: at the first character that is neither a space nor a digit — the full stop after 67, or the comma after 33. Its weakness: it never counts digits, so +7 701 123 45 67 2026 жылға gives +7 701 123 45 67 2026 — the year gets swallowed. \+7(?:[\s-]?\d){10} counts ten digits and stops in the right place.

Production-grade validation would need stricter rules — this pattern happily accepts nonsense like +7 999999999999.
Example 02 · Years

Extract years

Қазақстан тәуелсіздігін 1991 жылы алды.
EXPO 2017 Астанада өтті.

Pattern: \b\d{4}\b

  • 1991
  • 2017

Boundary + class + quantifier: three pieces, one useful pattern.

Piece by piece
\b
a word boundary — the position between a word character and a non-word character
\d{4}
exactly four digits
\b
another word boundary, on the other side

In words: four digits standing on their own.

Why the boundaries matter: without them, \d{4} applied to нөмір 12345 happily returns 1234 — the first four digits of a longer number. With \b on both sides there is no match at all, which is what you want when you are looking for years.

Example 03 · Dates

Extract dates

Кездесу 24.08.2026 күні Астанада өтеді.

Pattern: \b\d{2}\.\d{2}\.\d{4}\b

  • 24.08.2026
Piece by piece
\b
word boundary, so we do not start in the middle of a longer number
\d{2}
the day — exactly two digits
\.
an escaped dot: the separator itself
\d{2}
the month
\.
the second separator
\d{4}
the year — exactly four digits
\b
closing word boundary

In words: two digits, dot, two digits, dot, four digits — the Kazakhstan date format.

The classic mistake: writing \d{2}.\d{2}.\d{4} without escaping. A bare . matches any character, so that pattern also accepts 24x08y2026. Also note: the pattern checks the shape, not the calendar — 99.99.2026 would pass.

Example 04 · Time

Extract times of day

Кездесу сағат 15:00-де, кеші 9:05-те аяқталады.

Pattern: \b(?:[01]?\d|2[0-3]):[0-5]\d\b

  • 15:00
  • 9:05

Alternation inside a non-capturing group lets us say “00–19 or 20–23”, so 25:99 is rejected.

Piece by piece
(?:
a non-capturing group holding the two hour options
[01]?\d
an optional 0 or 1, then any digit → covers 0–9 and 00–19
|
or
2[0-3]
a 2 followed by 0–3 → covers 20–23
:
the literal colon
[0-5]\d
minutes: first digit 0–5, then any digit → 00–59

In words: an hour from 0 to 23, a colon, then minutes from 00 to 59.

What the ranges buy you: a lazy \d{1,2}:\d{2} would also accept 25:99. Here the alternation encodes the real rule, so 25:99 is rejected while both 15:00 and the single-digit 9:05 are accepted.

Example 05 · Vehicle plates

Kazakhstan vehicle plates

123 ABC 01
777 KZK 02

Pattern: \d{3}\s[A-Z]{3}\s\d{2}

  • 123 ABC 01
  • 777 KZK 02

Real plate formats and their validation rules are more complex than this; the pattern shows fixed-length groups.

Piece by piece
\d{3}
exactly three digits
\s
exactly one whitespace character
[A-Z]{3}
exactly three capital Latin letters
\s
another single space
\d{2}
exactly two digits — the region code

In words: three digits, space, three capital letters, space, two digits.

Detail: {3} is strict — no more, no less. And [A-Z] is Latin only, so a plate typed with Cyrillic А or В (which look identical on screen) will not match. Confusable letters are a genuine, everyday problem in Kazakhstan text data.

Example 06 · Hashtags

Find hashtags

Бүгін #Astana қаласында #AI туралы конференция өтті.

Pattern: #\w+

  • #Astana
  • #AI

For Kazakh hashtags use #[^\s#]+ — see the anchors section.

Piece by piece
#
a literal hash character
\w
one word character — a letter, a digit or an underscore
+
one or more of them, so the tag runs until the first space or punctuation

In words: a hash, then the run of word characters after it.

The Kazakh catch: in JavaScript \w is only [A-Za-z0-9_], so #Астана is missed entirely. Use #[^\s#]+ (“a hash, then anything that is not a space or another hash”), or switch on the u flag and write #[\p{L}\d_]+.

Example 07 · Cleaning

Clean extra spaces

Алматы     Қазақстанның    ең ірі қалаларының бірі.

Pattern: \s+ — replacement: " " 

Алматы Қазақстанның ең ірі қалаларының бірі.

Normalising whitespace is one of the very first steps of almost every text-processing pipeline.

Piece by piece
\s
one whitespace character — a space, a tab, or a newline
+
one or more in a row, so a run of ten spaces is a single match
" "
the replacement: exactly one ordinary space

In words: replace every run of whitespace with a single space.

Why + matters here: without it you would replace each space with a space and change nothing. With it, the whole run collapses at once. In Python this is re.sub(r"\s+", " ", text), and .strip() afterwards removes the leftover space at the start and end.

Example 08 · Emails

Extract email addresses

Сұрақтарды info@example.kz адресіне жіберіңіз.

Pattern: [\w.-]+@[\w.-]+\.\w+

  • info@example.kz
Piece by piece
[\w.-]
one character that is a word character, a dot, or a hyphen
+
one or more of them — the part before the @
@
the literal at sign
[\w.-]+
the same set again — the domain name
\.
an escaped dot before the zone
\w+
the zone itself: kz, com, …

In words: some name characters, an @, some domain characters, a dot, and a zone.

Two details worth knowing. Inside [ ] the dot loses its special meaning, so it needs no backslash there — but outside the brackets it does. And the hyphen is written last on purpose: in the middle it would create a range, as in a-z.

This is not a complete, RFC-compliant email validator. It is good enough to find addresses in text, which is usually what an NLP pipeline actually needs.

    Code examples

    Practising in Python

    In the practical part of NLP work we use Python. Every output below is the real output of the code above it — run them yourself and change the text.

    1 · findall — every match at once

    import re

text = "Қазақстан 1991 жылы тәуелсіз болды. EXPO 2017 Астанада өтті."
years = re.findall(r"\b\d{4}\b", text)

print(years)
    ['1991', '2017']

    What happens here: findall scans the whole string and returns every match as a list of strings. If nothing matches you get an empty list, not None — so you can loop over the result safely. The gotcha: as soon as your pattern contains capturing brackets, findall returns the contents of the brackets instead of the whole match.

    2 · search + groups — take a match apart

    import re

text = "Кездесу 24.08.2026 күні өтеді."
m = re.search(r"(\d{2})\.(\d{2})\.(\d{4})", text)

print(m.group(0))
print(m.groups())
print("Year:", m.group(3))
    24.08.2026
('24', '08', '2026')
Year: 2026

    What happens here: search looks for the first match only and gives you a Match object — group(0) is everything that matched, group(1), group(2), … are the bracketed pieces, numbered by their opening bracket. Always check for None: if nothing matches, search returns None and m.group(0) raises AttributeError.

    3 · Named groups — readable code

    import re

text = "Курс 24.08.2026 күні басталады."
m = re.search(
    r"(?P<day>\d{2})\.(?P<month>\d{2})\.(?P<year>\d{4})",
    text
)

print(m.groupdict())
print(m.group("month"))
    {'day': '24', 'month': '08', 'year': '2026'}
08

    What happens here: (?P<day>…) gives a group a name, so the code says m.group("month") instead of m.group(2) — and stays correct even if you later insert another bracket. groupdict() hands you all named groups as a dictionary, ready to build a date object. Note the syntax difference: Python writes (?P<name>…), JavaScript writes (?<name>…).

    4 · sub — clean the text

    import re

text = "Алматы     Қазақстанның    ең ірі қалаларының бірі."
clean = re.sub(r"\s+", " ", text).strip()

print(clean)
    Алматы Қазақстанның ең ірі қалаларының бірі.

    What happens here: sub replaces every match, not just the first, and returns a new string — the original is untouched, because Python strings are immutable. \s+ collapses each run of whitespace into one space; .strip() then removes the space that may be left at the very start and end.

    5 · sub — remove punctuation before tokenizing

    import re

text = "Сәлем, әлем! Бүгін #NLP сабағы бар."
words_only = re.sub(r"[^\w\s]", "", text)

print(words_only)
    Сәлем әлем Бүгін NLP сабағы бар

    What happens here: [^\w\s] reads as “not a word character and not whitespace” — that is, punctuation and symbols. Replacing it with an empty string leaves words and the spaces between them. Why it works on Kazakh: Python’s \w is Unicode-aware, so Сәлем counts as word characters. The same line in JavaScript would delete every Cyrillic letter instead.

    This works on Kazakh text because Python’s \w is Unicode-aware. The same pattern in JavaScript would delete every Cyrillic letter — always check which engine you are in.

    6 · Prices — alternation and optional blocks

    import re

text = "Билет 1 500 теңге, кофе 990 тг, тағы бірі 12 000 теңге."
prices = re.findall(r"\d+(?:\s\d{3})*\s*(?:теңге|тг)", text)

print(prices)
    ['1 500 теңге', '990 тг', '12 000 теңге']

    What happens here: all the brackets are non-capturing, so findall returns whole prices rather than pieces of them. (?:\s\d{3})* is what makes 1 500 and 12 000 work while still matching plain 990 — “zero or more thousands blocks”.

    7 · finditer — matches with their positions

    import re

text = "Бүгін #Astana қаласында #AI туралы конференция өтті."

for m in re.finditer(r"#\w+", text):
    print(m.group(), m.start(), m.end())
    #Astana 6 13
#AI 24 27

    What happens here: finditer returns Match objects instead of strings, so you also get start() and end() — the character positions of each match. You need those whenever the position matters: highlighting a match in a page, replacing it in place, or storing an entity span for Named Entity Recognition, which is exactly the format NER training data uses.

    Positions matter as soon as you want to highlight a match, replace it in place, or label it for Named Entity Recognition.

    8 · split — cut text into sentences

    import re

text = "Астана — астана. Алматы ірі қала! Шымкент те үлкен?"
sentences = re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text)

print(sentences)
    ['Астана — астана.', 'Алматы ірі қала!', 'Шымкент те үлкен?']

    What happens here: split cuts the string wherever the pattern matches and returns the pieces. The lookbehind (?<=[.!?]) checks that a sentence mark is directly behind the current position without consuming it, so the punctuation stays attached to its sentence and only the space between sentences is used as the cut point.

    (?<=…) is a lookbehind: split after a ., ! or ? without consuming it. This naive splitter still breaks on abbreviations — real sentence splitting is a Lecture 2 topic.

    9 · compile + flags — reuse one pattern

    import re

nlp = re.compile(r"\bnlp\b", re.IGNORECASE)

print(nlp.findall("NLP, nlp, Nlp курсы"))
    ['NLP', 'nlp', 'Nlp']

    What happens here: re.compile builds the pattern once and gives you an object with the same methods — findall, search, sub. Use it when the same pattern runs over many texts; it also keeps the flags in one place. re.IGNORECASE makes NLP, nlp and Nlp all match.

    10 · A small pipeline

    Regex is rarely used alone — it is the cleaning step in front of everything else. 

    import re

raw = "  Сәлем!!!   Менің нөмірім +7 701 123 45 67,   #NLP  "

phones = re.findall(r"\+7[\s\d]+", raw)          # keep what we need
text = re.sub(r"\+7[\s\d]+", "<PHONE>", raw)     # then anonymise it
text = re.sub(r"\s+", " ", text).strip()         # normalise whitespace

print(phones)
print(text)
    ['+7 701 123 45 67']
Сәлем!!! Менің нөмірім <PHONE>, #NLP

    What happens here — and why in this order: first findall keeps a copy of the phone numbers, because the next line destroys them; then sub replaces each number with <PHONE>, which is how personal data is removed from a dataset before training; only then is the whitespace normalised, so the extra spaces left behind by the replacement disappear too. Swap the first two lines and you lose the numbers.

    That pattern is still fragile. [\s\d]+ means “spaces and digits, as many as possible”, so in +7 701 123 45 67 2026 жылға дейін it swallows the year as well and returns +7 701 123 45 67 2026 . A pattern that counts the digits — \+7(?:[\s-]?\d){10} — stops in the right place. Always look at what your Regex actually matched.
    The r"..." prefix marks a raw string, so the backslashes reach the Regex engine unchanged. Without it you would have to write "\\b\\d{4}\\b".
    In class

    Knowledge check

    Five questions to answer together before we finish.

    Question 1

    Read this pattern out loud, piece by piece: \b\d{2}\.\d{2}\.\d{4}\b

    Show Answer

    Word boundary · two digits · a literal dot · two digits · a literal dot · four digits · word boundary. In other words: a date written as 24.08.2026.

    Question 2

    What is the difference between \d+ and \d* ?

    Show Answer

    + requires at least one digit; * is happy with none, so it can match an empty string. Use * only for parts that really are allowed to be missing.

    Question 3

    Why does <.+> match too much in <b>Астана</b> және <b>Алматы</b> and how do you fix it?

    Show Answer

    + is greedy, so it runs to the last > in the text and returns one huge match. Make it lazy — <.+?> — and you get the four separate tags.

    Question 4

    In the playground, \bАстана\b finds nothing. Why?

    Show Answer

    The playground runs JavaScript, where \b is defined through the ASCII \w. A Cyrillic letter is not a word character there, so no boundary exists. Drop the \b, or use the u flag with \p{L}.

    Question 5

    Кеңсе: +7 7172 55 44 33, сағат 15:00-ден кейін. Which parts does \+7[\s\d]+ match, and why not the time?

    Show Answer

    It matches +7 7172 55 44 33. The pattern must start with a literal +7, and 15:00 does not — the colon is neither a space nor a digit either.

    Кездесу сағат 15:00-де, кеші 9:05-те аяқталады.

    Show Answer

    \d{1,2}:\d{2}

    15:00 9:05 — a stricter version that rejects 25:99 is \b(?:[01]?\d|2[0-3]):[0-5]\d\b.

    http://egov.kz https://astana.kz — the ? makes the s optional.

    Task 8 — Capitalised words

    Әлия Астанадан Алматыға ұшты, ал Қанат Шымкентте қалды.

    Show Answer

    [А-ЯЁӘҒҚҢӨҰҮҺІ][а-яёәғқңөұүһі]+

    Task 9 — Kazakh hashtags too

    Бүгін #Astana және #Астана туралы жаздық.

    Show Answer

    #[^\s#]+

    #Astana #Астана — “a #, then anything that is not a space or another #”. With the u flag, #[\p{L}\d_]+ is the precise version.

    Task 10 — Day, month and year separately

    Кездесу 24.08.2026 күні өтеді. Capture the three parts, not just the whole date.

    Show Answer

    (\d{2})\.(\d{2})\.(\d{4})

    Group 1 = 24, group 2 = 08, group 3 = 2026. In Python: m.group(3) or, with named groups, m.group("year").
