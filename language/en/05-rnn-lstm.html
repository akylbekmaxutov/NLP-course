Theory · The problem

What every model so far has thrown away

Bag of Words counted. TF-IDF weighted the counts. Lecture 4 averaged word vectors. All three are functions of which words appeared, never of where.

Four words, two meanings, one input. қосымша өте жақсы емес is a complaint; shuffling the same four words is not. Every representation we have built collapses them into the same row of numbers, so no classifier trained on top could ever separate them — the information was destroyed before the classifier ran. The difference of 3 × 10⁻⁸ is the order in which the floats were summed, nothing more.

This lecture is about the fix. Instead of collapsing the review into one point, we feed the words in one at a time and let the model keep a running summary. Order is then part of the computation rather than something discarded before it starts.

Theory · The model

What a recurrent network is

One small network, applied once per word, carrying a summary of everything it has read so far.

A recurrent neural network keeps a hidden state: a vector that starts at zero and is rewritten at every word, h = f(h, word). The same f and the same weights run at every position. After the last word, that one vector is the model's summary of the review.

The same network drawn four times, not four networks. Its weights are shared across positions, so a review of three words and one of forty go through exactly the same parameters.

Inside a single step

Everything happens in one line of arithmetic. The step takes two things — the state from the previous word and the current word — multiplies each by its own weight matrix, adds them together, and squashes the result.

One step of a plain RNN. W is applied to the word, U to the previous state; the two are added and squashed by tanh into the new state.

Reading hₜ = tanh(W·xₜ + U·hₜ₋₁ + b)
xₜ
the current word, as its embedding vector — 100 numbers in our model
hₜ₋₁
what the network knew after the previous word — 128 numbers
W, U
the two weight matrices. They are the same at every position: this is what "recurrent" means
tanh
squashes every number into (−1, 1), which stops the state from growing without limit
hₜ
the new state, which becomes hₜ₋₁ at the next word

Reading: there is no memory anywhere else. Whatever the network wants to remember about the first word has to survive inside h, through every rewrite that follows.

Watch the state being passed

The clearest way to see it is to run the recurrence by hand and check the answer against PyTorch. Below, a 4-dimensional state and four words — small enough that every number fits on the page. 

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)

# A tiny RNN so the numbers fit on the page: 3-dimensional words, 4-dimensional state.
rnn = nn.RNN(input_size=3, hidden_size=4, batch_first=True)

words = torch.tensor([[[1.0, 0.0, 0.0],    # қосымша
                       [0.0, 1.0, 0.0],    # өте
                       [0.0, 0.0, 1.0],    # жақсы
                       [1.0, 1.0, 0.0]]])  # емес

# --- what nn.RNN does, written out ----------------------------------------
W_ih, W_hh = rnn.weight_ih_l0, rnn.weight_hh_l0
b_ih, b_hh = rnn.bias_ih_l0, rnn.bias_hh_l0

h = torch.zeros(4)                                      # the state starts at zero
print("h0 (before any word):", h.numpy().round(3))
for step in range(4):
    x = words[0, step]
    h = torch.tanh(W_ih @ x + b_ih + W_hh @ h + b_hh)   # <- the whole recurrence
    print(f"h{step + 1} after word {step + 1}   :", h.detach().numpy().round(3))

# --- the same thing, done by PyTorch --------------------------------------
outputs, last = rnn(words)
print("\nPyTorch's last hidden state:", last[0, 0].detach().numpy().round(3))
print("matches our loop           :", bool(torch.allclose(h, last[0, 0], atol=1e-6)))
print("\noutputs holds every step   :", tuple(outputs.shape), "= (batch, words, hidden)")
print("its last row is the last hidden state:",
      bool(torch.allclose(outputs[0, -1], last[0, 0])))

Three things this shows. First, h₀ is all zeros — the network starts with no knowledge and builds everything from the words. Second, each column is computed only from the previous column and the current word, which is why order matters: feed the words in a different order and every column after the first changes. Third, nn.RNN is exactly the loop we wrote — the framework is a fast implementation, not a different algorithm.

Because the words arrive in order, жақсы емес and емес жақсы now produce different hidden states. That is the entire reason for the extra machinery: nothing else in the course could tell those two apart.

Theory · The fix

Why a plain RNN forgets, and what an LSTM does

The hidden state is rewritten at every single word. Rewrite it forty times and whatever was in it at the start is gone.

The vanishing gradient, in words

Training works backwards. The loss is computed at the end of the review, and to discover that the first word mattered, that signal has to travel back through every step in between. At each step it is multiplied by the same two things: the derivative of tanh, which is never greater than 1, and the recurrent matrix U. Multiply by roughly the same factor forty times and one of two things happens.

Factor below 1 — vanishing

0.540 is about 10−12. The signal arrives as nothing, the early weights never move, and the model simply never learns that the beginning of the review existed.

Factor above 1 — exploding

1.540 is about 107. One batch produces an enormous update and the weights jump to nonsense. This half is easy to patch, and clip_grad_norm_ in our training loop is the patch.

The vanishing gradient, measured

This is not a story — it is something you can print. Feed a 40-word sequence in, send one signal back from the final state, and ask how much of it reaches each input position. 

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

LENGTH, DIM, HIDDEN = 40, 32, 64

def gradient_by_position(cell):
    """How much does the final state still depend on the word at each position?"""
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    words = torch.randn(1, LENGTH, DIM, requires_grad=True)
    _, state = cell(words)
    hidden = state[0] if isinstance(state, tuple) else state   # LSTM returns (h, c)
    hidden.sum().backward()                    # one signal, sent back from the end
    return words.grad[0].norm(dim=1)           # one number per position

torch.manual_seed(0)
rnn = nn.RNN(DIM, HIDDEN, batch_first=True)
torch.manual_seed(0)
lstm = nn.LSTM(DIM, HIDDEN, batch_first=True)

# The standard trick: start the forget gate open. nn.LSTM stacks the gates as
# (input, forget, cell, output), so the forget bias is the second quarter.
torch.manual_seed(0)
open_lstm = nn.LSTM(DIM, HIDDEN, batch_first=True)
with torch.no_grad():
    open_lstm.bias_ih_l0[HIDDEN:2 * HIDDEN].fill_(3.0)      # sigmoid(3) ≈ 0.95

grads = {name: gradient_by_position(cell) for name, cell in
         (("RNN", rnn), ("LSTM", lstm), ("LSTM, forget open", open_lstm))}

print(f"{'steps back':>10} {'RNN':>10} {'LSTM':>10} {'LSTM, forget open':>19}")
for back in (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 39):
    i = LENGTH - 1 - back
    row = "  ".join(f"{g[i]:>8.1e}" for g in grads.values())
    print(f"{back:>10}   {row}")

print("\nhow much of the last word's signal still reaches the first word:")
for name, g in grads.items():
    ratio = float(g[0] / g[-1])
    per_step = ratio ** (1 / (LENGTH - 1))
    print(f"  {name:<18} {ratio:>9.1e}   ×{per_step:.2f} per step")

print("\nThe RNN loses half the signal at every step, so after 39 there is nothing")
print("left. The LSTM's default init is barely better. But hold the forget gate")
print("open and the factor becomes 1.00 — the signal survives the whole sequence.")
print("That knob is the entire point of the cell state, and an LSTM can learn it.")

The point: an LSTM does not remove the vanishing gradient. It gives the model a knob that can switch it off, and lets training decide, per dimension and per review, when to turn it. A plain RNN has no such knob.

What an LSTM adds

An LSTM adds a second vector, the cell state, which travels from step to step almost untouched — multiplied and added to, never passed through a weight matrix. Three gates, each a small network producing numbers between 0 and 1, decide what to erase, what to add, and what to reveal.

The blue line is the cell state. Follow it across: it meets one multiplication and one addition, and nothing else. That straight path is what the gradient travels back along.

The two equations, term by term
f ⊙ cₜ₋₁
forget. A number near 1 keeps that dimension of the memory; near 0 erases it. This is the knob measured above
i ⊙ ĉ
input. ĉ is the new candidate value, i decides how much of it to write
cₜ = f ⊙ cₜ₋₁ + i ⊙ ĉ
the whole update: scale what was there, add what is new. An addition, not a rewrite
hₜ = o ⊙ tanh(cₜ)
output. The memory and the visible state are now two different things — the cell can hold something without exposing it

Compare with the RNN: there, h was both the memory and the output, and every step overwrote it. Here the memory has its own protected lane.

The gates, printed

The same trick as before: write the equations out by hand, print the gate values at every step, and confirm against nn.LSTM. 

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)

lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=3, hidden_size=4, batch_first=True)
words = torch.randn(1, 6, 3)

# nn.LSTM stacks the four gates into one matrix, in this order:
i_w, f_w, g_w, o_w = lstm.weight_ih_l0.chunk(4)
i_u, f_u, g_u, o_u = lstm.weight_hh_l0.chunk(4)
i_b, f_b, g_b, o_b = (lstm.bias_ih_l0 + lstm.bias_hh_l0).chunk(4)

h = torch.zeros(4)
c = torch.zeros(4)
print(f"{'step':>4}  {'forget':>22}  {'input':>22}  {'cell state c':>26}")
for t in range(6):
    x = words[0, t]
    f = torch.sigmoid(f_w @ x + f_u @ h + f_b)      # what to keep from c
    i = torch.sigmoid(i_w @ x + i_u @ h + i_b)      # how much of the new value to add
    g = torch.tanh(g_w @ x + g_u @ h + g_b)         # the candidate value
    o = torch.sigmoid(o_w @ x + o_u @ h + o_b)      # what to reveal
    c = f * c + i * g                               # <- the highway: multiply, then add
    h = o * torch.tanh(c)                           # <- what the next layer sees
    print(f"{t + 1:>4}  {str(f.detach().numpy().round(2)):>22}"
          f"  {str(i.detach().numpy().round(2)):>22}"
          f"  {str(c.detach().numpy().round(2)):>26}")

_, (h_torch, c_torch) = lstm(words)
print("\nmatches nn.LSTM — h:", bool(torch.allclose(h, h_torch[0, 0], atol=1e-6)),
      " c:", bool(torch.allclose(c, c_torch[0, 0], atol=1e-6)))

Batching: why padding is needed at all

The network is fastest when it processes many reviews at once, because that turns per-word arithmetic into matrix multiplication. But a batch is a tensor, and a tensor is a rectangle: every row must have the same length. Reviews do not. So we pad each batch out to its longest review and pass the true lengths alongside.

Three decisions in collate
pad to the batch
not to a fixed width. A batch of short reviews stays narrow, so no work is wasted on a rectangle sized for the longest review in the dataset
pad with 0
index 0 is <pad>, and Embedding(..., padding_idx=0) keeps that row at zero and never trains it
keep the lengths
without them nothing downstream can tell a real word from filler

Reading: padding is a shape problem, not a modelling idea. Everything that follows exists to undo it.

Above: the rectangle, 15 cells for 9 real words. Below: the same words packed into a flat list, ordered by time step, with a count of how many reviews are still running at each step.

batch_sizes: [3, 2, 2, 1, 1]

read batch_sizes as: at time step 1 there are 3 reviews still running,
at step 2 there are 2, at steps 3, 4 and 5 only 1.
The network runs 5 steps and touches 9 cells instead of 15
What the two arrays mean
data
every real word, ordered by time step rather than by review: all the first words, then all the second words, and so on
batch_sizes
[3, 2, 2, 1, 1] — three reviews are still running at step 1, only one by step 4. The counts fall as short reviews finish
the result
5 time steps, 9 cells touched. The 6 padding cells are never computed at all

Reading: enforce_sorted=False is what lets us hand reviews over in any order — PyTorch sorts them internally and puts the results back in our order afterwards.

The class

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

PAD = 0

class Recurrent(nn.Module):
    """Embedding -> RNN or LSTM -> Linear."""

    def __init__(self, vocab_size, classes, kind="lstm", dim=100, hidden=128, dropout=0.3):
        super().__init__()
        self.embedding = nn.Embedding(vocab_size, dim, padding_idx=PAD)
        cell = nn.LSTM if kind == "lstm" else nn.RNN
        self.rnn = cell(dim, hidden, batch_first=True)
        self.dropout = nn.Dropout(dropout)
        self.out = nn.Linear(hidden, classes)

    def forward(self, ids, lengths):
        embedded = self.dropout(self.embedding(ids))          # (batch, words, dim)
        packed = nn.utils.rnn.pack_padded_sequence(
            embedded, lengths, batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False)
        _, state = self.rnn(packed)
        hidden = state[0] if isinstance(state, tuple) else state   # LSTM returns (h, c)
        return self.out(self.dropout(hidden[-1]))             # (batch, classes)

The five lines that do the learning
optimiser.zero_grad()
PyTorch accumulates gradients, so they must be cleared before each batch. Forgetting this is the most common beginner bug
model(ids, lengths)
the forward pass — class scores for every review in the batch
loss_fn(scores, labels)
CrossEntropyLoss takes raw scores, applies softmax itself, and gives one number to minimise
loss.backward()
the gradient of that number with respect to every parameter, computed automatically
optimiser.step()
Adam nudges each parameter in the direction that lowers the loss

And one guard: clip_grad_norm_ caps the gradient before the step. Recurrent networks are where gradients actually explode, and without it the RNN run can diverge into nan.

def split(name, per_class):
    df = load_dataset("issai/kazsandra", "score_classification", split=name,
                      token=os.getenv("HF_TOKEN")).to_pandas()
    per_class = min(per_class, df["label"].value_counts().min())   # 2 stars is the rarest
    df = pd.concat([g.sample(per_class, random_state=SEED) for _, g in df.groupby("label")])
    return df.sample(frac=1, random_state=SEED).reset_index(drop=True)

def tokenize(text):
    return re.findall(r"\w+", str(text).lower())

NAMES = ["1 star", "2 stars", "3 stars", "4 stars", "5 stars"]

    vocab[word] = len(vocab)

def encode(df, max_len=60):
    rows = []
    for text, label in zip(df["text_cleaned"], df["label"]):
        ids = [vocab.get(w, UNK) for w in tokenize(text)][:max_len]
        rows.append((ids or [UNK], int(label)))
    return rows

def collate(batch):
    lengths = torch.tensor([len(ids) for ids, _ in batch])
    padded = torch.full((len(batch), int(lengths.max())), PAD, dtype=torch.long)
    for i, (ids, _) in enumerate(batch):
        padded[i, :len(ids)] = torch.tensor(ids)
    return padded, lengths, torch.tensor([y for _, y in batch])

test_batches = DataLoader(test_rows, 64, shuffle=False, collate_fn=collate)

    return DataLoader(train_rows, 64, shuffle=True, collate_fn=collate,

            self.dropout(self.embedding(ids)), lengths, batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False)
        _, state = self.rnn(packed)
        hidden = state[0] if isinstance(state, tuple) else state
        return self.out(self.dropout(hidden[-1]))

    with torch.no_grad():

            right += int((model(ids, lengths).argmax(1) == labels).sum())

    model.eval()
    out, gold = [], []
    with torch.no_grad():
        for ids, lengths, labels in batches:
            out.append(model(ids, lengths).argmax(1))
            gold.append(labels)
    return torch.cat(out).numpy(), torch.cat(gold).numpy()

Accuracy is the wrong metric for an ordered scale. It charges the same penalty for calling a five-star review four stars as for calling it one star. When classes have an order — ratings, severity levels, grades — report the distance as well: mean absolute error, or the within-one accuracy above. A model that is never more than one star off is useful; its accuracy of 0.374 makes it sound useless.

And notice what is not the explanation. The test set is balanced at 490 per class, so this is not the imbalance of the raw data showing through. Five ordered classes on nine-word reviews is simply a harder problem than two.

Theory · The honest summary

What recurrence still cannot do

It reads strictly left to right

When the model reaches емес it cannot go back and revise what it thought about жақсы. A bidirectional LSTM reads both ways and helps, but it is two passes glued together, not a solution.

Everything squeezes through one vector

A forty-word review has to fit into 128 numbers before the classifier sees anything. The longer the text, the more that bottleneck costs.

It cannot be parallelised

Step 20 needs the result of step 19. Words cannot be processed simultaneously, which is exactly why training on very large corpora became impractical.

Distance still costs

Gates slow the forgetting, they do not stop it. Linking word 2 to word 45 means carrying information through 43 updates.

The next lecture removes the loop entirely. Instead of passing information along a chain, every word looks directly at every other word in one step — which fixes the bottleneck, the distance penalty and the parallelism problem at once. That is attention, and it is what Transformers are built from.

Knowledge check

Five questions we answer together before finishing.

Question 1

What can an RNN represent that an average of word vectors cannot?

Show Answer

Order. The hidden state after жақсы емес differs from the one after емес жақсы; the average is identical for both, as measured at the top of this page.

Question 2

Why is rnn.weight_ih_l0 shaped (512, 100) when the hidden size is 128?

Show Answer

512 = 4 × 128. An LSTM computes four things at each step — the forget, input and output gates plus the candidate value — and PyTorch stacks their weight matrices into one.

Question 3

The LSTM's training loss keeps falling while test accuracy stops moving. What is happening, and what would you change?

Show Answer

Overfitting — 700 000 parameters against 8 000 short reviews. Options: stop earlier, raise dropout, shrink the model, or give it more data. We measured the last one: 40 000 reviews took it from 0.772 to 0.803.

Question 4

Why does starting from the Lecture 4 word2vec help so much in the first epoch?

Show Answer

83% of the parameters are the embedding table, and 5 743 of its 5 916 rows arrive already knowing which words behave alike. The network starts from structure instead of noise: 0.785 after one epoch against 0.727.

Question 5

The score model is 0.374 accurate. Why is that not the most informative number to report?

Show Answer

The classes are ordered, and accuracy treats every mistake as equal. The same model is within one star 70.4% of the time and off by three or more only 12.2% of the time — which is what someone deciding whether to use it actually needs to know.
