Large Language Models

The same decoder you built in Lecture 6, made a thousand times larger and trained on most of the written internet. This lecture is about what that changes in practice: what the sizes mean, what the calls cost, which knobs you turn, and what you can still adapt without a GPU.

Theory · The mechanism

How an LLM works

There is less new machinery here than the name suggests. An LLM is the decoder stack from Lecture 6, scaled up, and trained in three stages instead of one.

A large language model is a Transformer decoder trained to predict the next token, at a scale where doing that one thing well turns out to produce translation, summarisation, code and conversation as side effects.

The loop, which you have already built

Predict one token, append it, predict again. Lecture 6 built this; the only difference now is the size of the box in the middle.

What is the same as Lecture 6, and what is not
the same
Q, K and V. Scaled dot-product attention. Multi-head. Residuals. A per-token feed-forward network. Causal masking. Next-token prediction with cross-entropy. You can read the source of any open model and recognise all of it
bigger
Lecture 6’s model was 2 blocks and 1 million parameters. A modern LLM is 32 to 126 blocks and 7 to 1 000 billion. The context window went from 60 tokens to hundreds of thousands
modernised
Pre-norm, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, grouped-query attention — the six substitutions from the end of Lecture 6. None of them changes the story
trained in three stages, not one
This is the genuinely new part, and the next figure is about it

So why does it feel different? Because at sufficient scale, “predict the next token” stops being a text-completion trick. To predict the next token of a chemistry paper you need some chemistry; to predict the next line of a proof you need some arithmetic. The objective did not change. What it takes to do the objective well did.

The three stages

Stage 1 buys knowledge and costs almost everything. Stages 2 and 3 buy behaviour and cost comparatively nothing.

The stages, one at a time
1. pretraining — the expensive one
Next-token prediction on trillions of tokens of scraped text. Self-supervised, exactly like Lecture 7’s masked language modelling, except causal. This is where the knowledge comes from, it costs millions of dollars, and it is done once by a lab with a data centre
2. instruction tuning (SFT) — making it answer
Supervised fine-tuning on pairs of (instruction, good answer) written by people. A base model asked a question tends to produce more questions, because that is what follows a question in web text. SFT is what makes it respond instead of continue. This is the stage InstructGPT introduced and it is why “instruct” appears in so many model names
3. preference tuning — making it better
People are shown two answers and pick the better one. The model is trained to prefer what people preferred. RLHF, DPO and their relatives all live here, and they are the subject of a later section

Names you will see on model cards: a model ending in -base has had stage 1 only — useful for further training, awkward to chat with. -instruct or -chat has had stages 2 and usually 3, and is what you actually want to call.

Practice · Arithmetic you can check

What “7B” actually means

Model names carry a number: 0.5B, 7B, 70B, 405B. It is the count of trainable numbers in the file. Turning it into gigabytes is one multiplication, and it decides everything about where the model can run.

A parameter is one number the training process adjusted. In Lecture 6 you counted them yourself: every entry of W_Q, W_K, W_V, Wₒ, the feed-forward matrices, the embedding table. “7B” means seven billion such numbers.

Reading the table
the rule of thumb
fp16 needs about 2 GB per billion parameters. A 7B model is ~14 GB, a 70B model ~140 GB. If you remember one number from this lecture, make it this one
precision is a dial, not a fact
The same model is 28 GB or 3.5 GB depending only on how many bits each weight is stored in. That is what quantization is, and it gets its own section below
the laptop line is real
A 7B model in fp16 fits in 24 GB of unified memory with room to work. A 70B model does not fit at any precision on this machine. This is the single most useful thing to know before planning a project
training needs far more than inference
Running a model needs the weights. Training it needs the weights, the gradients, and the optimiser state — with AdamW, roughly 12–16 bytes per parameter instead of 2. A 7B model you can run on a laptop needs about 100 GB to fully fine-tune

That last row is why LoRA exists, and why this lecture can show you the code but not run it.

The KV cache, which is the real constraint

The weights are loaded once and shared by everyone. The KV cache — the stored keys and values from Lecture 6, kept so that generating token t+1 does not have to re-read the whole prompt — is per user, per token, and it is what actually runs out.

This is why long context is expensive, why providers charge for input tokens at all, and why grouped-query attention from Lecture 6 exists — it shrinks precisely this table by sharing K and V across query heads.
Theory · The landscape

Open-weight and proprietary models

“Open-source LLM” is used loosely and usually incorrectly. There are three distinct things, and the difference matters when a lawyer asks.

Three levels, routinely collapsed into one word. The middle box is where most of the ecosystem lives.

The three, precisely
proprietary / closed
You send text to an endpoint and get text back. The weights never leave the provider. Examples: the GPT, Claude and Gemini families. You pay per token, you get the strongest models, and you cannot inspect, host, or keep them
open-weight
The trained weights are published and you can download and run them. The training data and the code that produced them usually are not. Examples: the Llama, Qwen, Mistral and Gemma families, and the Kazakh models from Lecture 7. This is what nearly everyone means by “open-source LLM”, and it is not the same thing
fully open
Weights, data, training code and a permissive licence. Examples are rarer: OLMo and the BLOOM line were built explicitly this way. Only here can an independent party actually reproduce or audit the model

Read the licence, not the marketing. Some open-weight licences are genuinely permissive (Apache 2.0, MIT). Others are custom and carry conditions — usage restrictions, a user-count threshold above which you must negotiate, or a clause about naming. “Free to download” and “free to use in your product” are different statements.

The same meaning, priced three ways. The improvement from cl100k to o200k is real and deliberate — but Kazakh still costs double.

Why this happens, and what to do
the cause is the vocabulary
A tokenizer's fixed vocabulary is bought with training text. English got most of it, so an English word is usually one token. Kazakh got very little, so Kazakh words are assembled from fragments — exactly the effect Lecture 7 measured on кітаптарымыздан
it is a tax on three things at once
You pay more per message, you fit less into the context window, and you wait longer, because generation time is per token. The same product is simply more expensive to run in Kazakh
it is improving
cl100k charged Kazakh 4.53×; o200k charges 2.00×. Newer tokenizers allocate vocabulary to more languages deliberately, and the gap has roughly halved in one generation
you cannot fix it from outside
The tokenizer ships with the model. Your only levers are choosing a model whose tokenizer handles your language better, and measuring before you commit

Do the measurement before the contract. Tokenize a realistic sample of your own traffic with each candidate’s tokenizer and compare. It takes minutes and it changes the budget by a factor of two.

Computing the bill

Every proprietary API prices the same way, with two rates: 

cost = (input_tokens  / 1 000 000) × price_in
     + (output_tokens / 1 000 000) × price_out

input_tokens  = system prompt + conversation history + this message
output_tokens = what the model generates
Four things people get wrong
output costs about five times input
Generating is sequential and cannot be batched the way reading can. A verbose system prompt is cheap; a verbose model is not. “Answer in at most three sentences” is a budget instruction
the whole conversation is resent every turn
The API is stateless. Turn 20 of a chat pays for turns 1–19 again as input. A long conversation costs quadratically in its own length unless you truncate or summarise
the system prompt is charged every single call
A 2 000-token system prompt on a million calls is two billion input tokens. This is what prompt caching is for: providers charge a fraction for a prefix they have already seen
reasoning tokens are output tokens
On a reasoning model, the thinking the model does before answering is billed as output, at the output rate, even when it is not shown to you

The three discounts worth knowing: prompt caching for a repeated prefix; batch processing for work that can wait (commonly around half price); and a smaller model for the easy share of your traffic.

Know which half of your bill you are trying to cut before you try to cut it.
Practice · The decision

How to choose a model

Benchmark tables are the least useful input to this decision. Four constraints usually settle it before quality is even discussed.

Residency and volume decide the branch; a twenty-example hand-built eval decides the model.

The questions, in the order they bind
1. where may the data go?
If personal, medical, banking or state data cannot leave the country or the building, the proprietary-API branch is closed and no amount of benchmark advantage reopens it. Decide this first, because it eliminates half the options for free
2. what is the volume, and is it steady?
A GPU costs the same whether you send it one request a day or a million. An API costs nothing when idle. Low or spiky traffic favours an API; steady high traffic favours self-hosting, and the crossover is an arithmetic question you can answer with the pricing section above
3. how good does it have to be, measured on what?
Write twenty real examples from your own problem with the answers you want. Run every candidate. This is a morning of work and it outranks every public leaderboard, which was measured on a task that is not yours — usually in English
4. what is the latency budget?
Generation is sequential: time scales with output tokens. A reasoning model that thinks for thirty seconds is unusable in an autocomplete box and fine in a nightly report
5. only then: which is smartest?
Start with the cheapest model that passes your twenty examples. Move up only where it demonstrably fails. Most production traffic is easy, and routing the easy share to a small model is the largest cost lever there is

For Kazakh, add one step before all of these: check the tokenizer, and check that the model can actually write the language. Lecture 7’s fill-in-the-blank test takes two minutes and disqualifies candidates faster than anything else.

Practice · Measured

Sampling parameters

At every step the model produces a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary. Sampling parameters decide how a token is picked from it. They do not change what the model knows — only how adventurously it chooses.

Temperature rescales the whole distribution; top-k and top-p truncate it. They are usually combined.

What each parameter did
greedy — always the most likely token
Identical across all three seeds, because nothing random happens. Use it when you want reproducibility: extraction, classification, anything you will compare run to run
temperature — divide the scores before the softmax
The entropy table is the whole story: 0.00 at T=0.1, 0.14 at T=1.0, 8.31 at T=2.0. Below 1 sharpens the distribution toward the top token; above 1 flattens it until the tail becomes reachable. At 1.5 this model produced fragments of Chinese, Japanese and Russian in an English sentence — the tail is where the vocabulary’s other 100 languages live
top-k — keep the k best tokens
top_k=1 is exactly greedy, which is a useful thing to have seen. The weakness of top-k is that k is fixed: when the model is certain, 50 candidates include 49 bad ones; when it is genuinely unsure, 50 may cut off good ones
top-p (nucleus) — keep the smallest set summing to p
Adapts to the model’s confidence: when one token has 0.99, the nucleus is one token; when the top forty are all plausible, it keeps forty. This is why top-p is the more common default

Sensible starting points: extraction and classification — greedy, or temperature 0. Chat and general assistance — temperature 0.7 with top-p 0.9. Creative writing — temperature 0.9–1.0. Above about 1.2 most models degrade quickly, as the measurement above shows.

Do not use temperature to fix a wrong answer. It is a choice among what the model already considered likely. If the correct answer is not in the distribution, no setting will find it. Fixing wrong answers is the job of the prompt, of retrieval (Lecture 9), or of a better model.

The finding this experiment actually produced

Read the outputs again for content rather than style. Almaty is not the capital of Kazakhstan — Astana is. Almaty is not near the Caspian Sea, not on the Amur River, not in the Russian Federation, and “Kars Oblast” does not exist. A 0.5 B model stated all of these fluently and with no hedging, at every temperature setting we tried.
Why this matters more than the parameters
fluency and accuracy are separate things
Every one of those sentences is grammatical, confident and well-formed. Nothing in the text signals that the content is invented. This is what people mean by hallucination, and it is not a bug in the sampler — greedy decoding produced the wrong capital just as readily
small models know less
0.5 B parameters is not much room for world knowledge. Larger models get this particular question right. But “larger” moves the boundary; it does not remove it
the gap is worst where the training data is thinnest
Lecture 7 found mBERT ranking Алматы above Астана for the capital. Here a generative model does the same thing in prose. Facts about Kazakhstan are exactly the facts most under-represented in an English-dominated pretraining corpus, which is precisely where a Kazakhstan-facing product needs them right
what to do about it
Do not ask a model to recall facts it may not have. Give it the facts in the prompt and ask it to use them — that is retrieval-augmented generation, and it is the next lecture

This is why the course measures things. We set out to demonstrate sampling parameters and accidentally produced a much more important lesson, because we read the output instead of trusting it.

Theory · A newer model class

Reasoning models

Since 2024 a second kind of model has appeared alongside the ordinary chat model. The difference is not the architecture. It is how much computation the model is allowed to spend before it answers.

A reasoning model generates a long internal chain of thought before its visible answer, and has been trained — usually with reinforcement learning — to make that chain actually useful: to plan, check itself, notice mistakes, and backtrack.

Same architecture, same next-token loop. The model has simply been trained to use a scratchpad first, and to use it well.

What is actually going on
test-time compute
The older way to make a model better was to make it bigger, which costs money once, at training. The newer way is to let it think for longer when the question arrives, which costs money every time. Accuracy on hard problems rises with the length of the chain — you are buying quality with tokens at inference
chain of thought was the precursor
Adding “think step by step” to a prompt improved arithmetic on ordinary models years earlier. Reasoning models internalise that: they do it without being asked, at much greater length, and they were trained on whether the final answer was right
trained by reinforcement learning on checkable problems
Maths and code have an objective answer, so a machine can score attempts without a human. Reward what reaches the right answer, and useful habits — checking, trying another approach — emerge without anyone specifying them. The next section is about that mechanism
you are usually shown a summary, not the chain
Providers commonly hide or summarise the raw reasoning. You are billed for it either way, which is the single most common budget surprise with these models

When to reach for one: multi-step arithmetic, code that must compile, logical constraints, planning. When not to: summarisation, classification, extraction, rewriting, translation, chat — anything where the answer is a transformation of the input rather than a derivation from it. For those, the thinking is latency and cost you get nothing for.

An effort dial is becoming standard. Rather than two separate model families, providers increasingly expose one model with a setting for how hard to think — low for routine traffic, high when correctness matters more than cost. Treat it as exactly what it is: a spend control, to be tuned per route and measured, not set once globally.

Theory · The third training stage

Reinforcement learning, and what it is for

Supervised learning needs a correct answer to imitate. For “write a helpful reply” there is no single correct answer — but people can reliably say which of two replies is better. Reinforcement learning is how that weaker signal gets turned into a better model.

RLHF in three steps. DPO collapses steps 2 and 3 into one supervised objective, which is why most teams now start there.

The vocabulary you will meet
RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback
The original recipe from InstructGPT: collect comparisons, fit a reward model, optimise the LLM against it with PPO while a KL penalty keeps it near where it started. It works and it is fiddly — three models in memory, an unstable optimiser, and a reward model that can be gamed
reward hacking
The failure mode that makes the KL penalty non-optional. The model finds text that scores well without being good — flattery, hedging, padding, restating the question. If you have noticed assistants that open with “Great question!”, you have seen a reward model’s taste, faithfully learned
DPO — direct preference optimisation
The observation that you can skip the reward model entirely: derive a loss that directly raises the probability of the preferred answer and lowers the other, relative to a frozen reference model. One model to train, an ordinary supervised loop, far easier to get right. This is where most teams start today
RLVR — RL from verifiable rewards
For maths, code and anything with a checkable answer, the reward is not a human or a learned model but a program: run the tests, check the answer. No human labelling, no reward model to hack, and a signal that stays honest at scale. This is the engine behind reasoning models

The shape of the whole idea: supervised learning teaches a model to imitate one good answer. Reinforcement learning teaches it to prefer better answers over worse ones. The second is a much weaker signal per example and a much better match for what “good” means in language.

Where this touches you. You are unlikely to run RLHF. But three of its consequences are things you will meet within a week of building anything: the model’s tone and refusals come from stage 3, not from its knowledge; “helpful” is a learned preference and not a fact, so different vendors’ models disagree about it; and the habit of padding an answer is a trained behaviour you can usually suppress with an explicit instruction.
Theory · Adapting a model

Fine-tuning: full-parameter and LoRA

Lecture 7 fine-tuned every weight of a 178 M encoder on a laptop. The same move on a 7 B model needs about a hundred gigabytes of accelerator memory. LoRA is how that number comes back down.

Nothing in this section is trained here. You asked for the recipes rather than the run, and that is the right call: none of these jobs fit on a laptop. What is measured below is the part that needs no GPU — the parameter counts, which are the whole argument.

Why full fine-tuning is expensive

The weights are the smallest part of a training job. The optimiser state is the largest, and LoRA removes almost all of it.

What LoRA does

Freeze the pretrained weight matrix W. Add a small detour beside it, and train only the detour. 

ordinary layer   h = W x                     W is (d, k), all of it trainable

LoRA layer       h = W x  +  (B · A) x       W frozen
                 A is (r, k), B is (d, r),   only A and B are trained
                 r is small: 4, 8, 16, 64

trainable numbers:  d·k      ->     r·(d + k)

The update a fine-tune needs turns out to be low-rank, so it can be stored as two thin matrices instead of one fat one.

         4       540,672       0.109%         2.2
         8     1,081,344       0.219%         4.3
        16     2,162,688       0.438%         8.7
        64     8,650,752       1.751%        34.6
Reading the table
0.219% at rank 8
One thousandth of the model, near enough. The optimiser state, which is what actually fills the memory, shrinks by the same factor
the adapter is 4 MB
Against roughly 1 GB for the base model in bf16. You can keep twenty task-specific adapters and swap them against one loaded base model — which is exactly how a provider offers fine-tuning to many customers at once
rank is the one real hyperparameter
Low rank (4–8) for style, tone and format. Higher (16–64) when the task needs knowledge the base model does not have. Higher rank is not automatically better and it costs memory linearly
no inference cost
B·A has the same shape as W, so it can be folded in after training. The deployed model is exactly as fast as the original

QLoRA goes one step further: hold the frozen base model in 4-bit and train LoRA adapters on top. That is what puts fine-tuning a 7 B model inside a single 16 GB consumer GPU, and it is the standard recipe for people without a cluster.

What the code looks like

This is the whole of a LoRA fine-tune. It is shown, not run — on the hardware this course uses it would not finish. 

from datasets import load_dataset
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

BASE = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(BASE)

# 1. the base model, frozen weights in bf16 (or 4-bit, for QLoRA)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(BASE, dtype="bfloat16", device_map="auto")

# 2. attach the adapters — this is the entire difference from Lecture 7
model = get_peft_model(model, LoraConfig(
    r=8,                      # rank: the width of the bottleneck
    lora_alpha=16,            # scaling, conventionally 2 x r
    lora_dropout=0.05,
    bias="none",
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
    target_modules=["q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj"],
))
model.print_trainable_parameters()     # -> trainable: 0.2% of all params

# 3. the data: instruction / response pairs in the model's own chat format
def format_example(row):
    messages = [{"role": "user", "content": row["instruction"]},
                {"role": "assistant", "content": row["response"]}]
    text = tokenizer.apply_chat_template(messages, tokenize=False)
    return tokenizer(text, truncation=True, max_length=1024)

data = load_dataset("json", data_files="kazakh_support_pairs.jsonl")["train"]
data = data.map(format_example, remove_columns=data.column_names)

# 4. an ordinary training loop — the same five operations as Lecture 5
#    (forward, loss, backward, clip, step). Nothing here is LoRA-specific.

# 5. save only the adapter: a few megabytes, not a few gigabytes
model.save_pretrained("adapters/kazakh-support")
The decisions that actually matter
target_modules
Which matrices get an adapter. The attention projections are the usual choice; adding the feed-forward matrices gives the adapter more capacity and more parameters. Start with attention only
the chat template
Training data must be formatted exactly as the model expects at inference, special tokens included. apply_chat_template does this; hand-rolling the format is the most common way a LoRA run silently produces a worse model
how much data
A few hundred to a few thousand well-chosen examples is the normal range for style and format. Lecture 7 measured the shape of this curve directly: the first thousand examples carry most of the gain
what fine-tuning is for, and not for
For: format, tone, a domain vocabulary, a task the model keeps getting structurally wrong, a language it handles poorly. Not for: teaching it facts. Facts belong in the prompt, retrieved at query time — which is Lecture 9

The honest order of operations: try a better prompt first, then few-shot examples, then retrieval, and only then fine-tune. Fine-tuning is the most expensive of the four and the one people reach for first.

Practice · Measured

Quantization

Every weight in a model is a number stored in some number of bits. Quantization stores it in fewer. It is the single most effective way to fit a model on hardware you actually own.

Quantization replaces high-precision weights with low-precision ones — typically 16-bit floats with 8-bit or 4-bit integers — keeping a small scale factor per row so the original values can be approximately reconstructed. 

for each row of the weight matrix:
    scale = max(|row|) / 127
    stored = round(row / scale)        an int8 in [-127, 127]
    restored = stored × scale          close to the original, not equal

one float32 weight  = 32 bits
one int8 weight     =  8 bits + a shared scale per row  ->  ~4x smaller
python3 lesson8.py --quantize
  Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct: 494,032,768 parameters
  precision       bytes/param       total   vs fp32
  --------------------------------------------------
  fp32                      4     1.98 GB      1.0x
  fp16/bf16                 2     0.99 GB      2.0x
  int8                      1     0.49 GB      4.0x
  int4                    0.5     0.25 GB      8.0x

  one real weight matrix, (896, 896), rounded to int8:
    original range      -1.2266 .. +1.1719
    mean absolute error 0.000484
    worst single error  0.004829
    relative error      1.15%
    distinct values     4,209 -> 255

  rounded 169 linear layers to int8 and back:
    «The capital of Kazakhstan is»
    fp32 : located in the ___ A. Central Asia region B
    int8 : located in the ___ A. Central Asia region B
    identical: True

Four times less memory, a one-percent perturbation of the weights, and an output that did not change at all.

Why this works so well
neural networks are robust to small weight noise
Training never found one exact set of numbers; it found a broad region where the loss is low. A 1% perturbation lands somewhere else in that region. This is also why dropout works
the win is memory bandwidth, not just capacity
Generation is limited by how fast weights can be read from memory, not by arithmetic. Quarter-sized weights means roughly four times less to read per token, which is why quantized models are also faster with real integer kernels
4-bit is where judgement is needed
int8 is close to free. int4 saves another factor of two and starts to cost measurable quality, more so on small models. The practical rule: a 4-bit 13 B model usually beats an 8-bit 7 B one at the same memory budget
not all layers are equal
Real schemes — GPTQ, AWQ, GGUF’s k-quants — keep sensitive layers at higher precision and use calibration data to choose scales. Our uniform per-row rounding is the teaching version; production quantizers are more careful and lose less

The honest caveat on our own demo: one prompt, identical output, is evidence that nothing broke — not proof that nothing changed. A real evaluation runs a benchmark before and after. Note also that the 0.5 B model’s answer is poor in both cases; quantization preserved the quality it had, which was not much.

What this buys you in practice. A 70 B model is 140 GB in fp16 and out of reach. At 4-bit it is 35 GB and runs on a single 80 GB accelerator, or on two consumer cards. Quantization is what moved the frontier of “models an individual can run” from about 7 B to about 70 B without anybody training anything new.

Theory · The honest summary

What an LLM still cannot do for you

It does not know your facts

We watched a model invent a Caspian coastline for Almaty. Anything specific to your organisation, or recent, or thinly represented in the training data, has to be supplied rather than recalled.

It cannot tell you when it is wrong

Fluency is uniform whether the content is right or invented. There is no signal in the text itself, which is why verification has to come from outside the model.

Your language may cost double

Measured here: 2.0× the tokens for Kazakh on the best tokenizer we tested, 4.5× on an older one. That is budget, latency and context window, all at once.

Context is finite and expensive

The KV cache table showed one 128k-token conversation needing more memory than the model itself. Long context is a real cost, not a free feature.

It is not deterministic by default

Anything you intend to test or audit needs greedy decoding and a fixed seed, or you are comparing samples rather than systems.

Fine-tuning is the last resort, not the first

Prompt, then examples, then retrieval, then fine-tune. Most problems people try to fine-tune away are prompt or retrieval problems.

  • TF-IDF→
  • word2vec→
  • LSTM→
  • Transformer→
  • pretrained encoder→
  • LLM→
  • LLM + your data

Every limitation in the grid above except the last two points the same way: the model needs to be given the right information at the moment it answers, instead of being asked to remember it. That is retrieval-augmented generation, and together with prompting and agents it is the next lecture.

In class

Knowledge check

Eight questions we answer together before finishing.

Question 1

A base model is asked “What is the capital of Kazakhstan?” and replies with another question. Is it broken?

Show Answer

No — it is doing exactly what stage 1 trained it to do: continue text. In web text, a question is often followed by more questions. Instruction tuning is the stage that turns a continuer into an assistant, and a -base checkpoint has not had it.

Question 2

How much memory does a 13B model need in fp16, and roughly what would full fine-tuning need?

Show Answer

About 26 GB to run (2 GB per billion). Full fine-tuning needs weights plus gradients plus two AdamW states — roughly 12–16 bytes per parameter, so on the order of 160–200 GB. That gap is the entire reason LoRA exists.

Question 3

Is Llama an open-source model?

Show Answer

It is open-weight. You can download and run the weights, but the training data and training code are not published, and the licence carries conditions. “Open-source” properly requires all of it. The distinction matters the first time a lawyer reads your architecture document.

Question 4

The same support message costs 19 tokens in English and 38 in Kazakh. Name three things that get worse, not one.

Show Answer

The bill roughly doubles; the usable context window roughly halves, because it is measured in tokens; and responses are slower, because generation time is per token. One tokenizer property, three separate costs.

Question 5

Your chatbot gives a wrong answer. Someone suggests lowering the temperature. Will that fix it?

Show Answer

No. Temperature only reweights tokens the model already considered likely. If the right answer is not in the distribution, no sampling setting reaches it. We measured this: greedy decoding — temperature effectively zero — still said Almaty was the capital. The fixes are the prompt, retrieval, or a different model.

Question 6

When is a reasoning model the wrong choice?

Show Answer

When the answer is a transformation of the input rather than a derivation from it: summarisation, classification, extraction, translation, rewriting. The thinking tokens are billed as output and add latency, and they buy nothing on tasks with no reasoning to do.

Question 7

LoRA at rank 8 trained 0.219% of the parameters. Why does that save so much more than 0.219% of the memory?

Show Answer

Because the frozen weights still have to be held, but gradients and optimiser state — which are the largest part of a training job, at 8–12 bytes per trainable parameter — now exist only for the adapter. You still pay for the model; you stop paying for the training machinery around it.

Question 8

Rounding 169 layers to int8 changed the weights by 1.15% and the output not at all. Does that prove int8 is free?

Show Answer

No. One prompt producing an identical continuation is evidence that nothing broke, not proof that nothing changed. A real check runs a benchmark before and after. It is fair to say int8 is close to free in practice, and that int4 is where measurable quality loss begins.

After the lecture

Practice tasks

None of these need a GPU. Most are a command and a written observation; two are arithmetic you should be able to do on paper by the end.

Task 1 — Price your own product

Take 100 real messages from something you are building. Count tokens with two different tokenizers, apply current published rates, and produce a monthly figure in Kazakh and in English.

Show Answer

The output is one number and one sentence: what it costs, and what the language penalty is on your own text. This is the single most useful hour in this lecture, and it is the thing a manager will actually ask for.

Task 2 — Find the crossover

At what monthly volume does renting a GPU beat paying per token? Pick a real GPU price and a real API rate and solve for the break-even.

Show Answer

Fixed monthly GPU cost divided by cost per call. The interesting part is what you must assume to get there: utilisation, tokens per call, and whether the smaller self-hosted model is good enough. State those assumptions; they matter more than the arithmetic.

Task 3 — Break a model with temperature

Run --sampling with temperature 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0. Find the point where output stops being usable, and describe what fails first.

Show Answer

Grammar usually survives longer than factuality, and the first visible failure on a multilingual model is often tokens from other languages appearing mid-sentence — the tail of the distribution being sampled. Ours broke between 1.0 and 1.5.

Task 4 — Hunt for hallucinations in Kazakh

Ask a small model ten factual questions about Kazakhstan. Record how many are wrong and how confident the wording sounds.

Show Answer

Expect a high error rate and uniformly confident phrasing. The deliverable is the pair of columns — correctness and apparent confidence — because the absence of correlation between them is the entire point.

Task 5 — Size a deployment

You must serve 50 concurrent users at 8k context on a 13B model. Compute weights plus KV cache and name the hardware.

Show Answer

Weights ~26 GB in fp16. KV cache scales with layers, KV heads, head dimension, context and users — use the formula from the memory section. Then redo it at 4-bit weights and with grouped-query attention, and note which change helped more.

Task 6 — Rank sweep

Run --lora and extend the table to ranks 1, 2, 128 and 256. Plot trainable parameters against rank.

Show Answer

The line is exactly straight: trainable parameters are linear in r. Nothing about LoRA is mysterious once you have drawn this — r is simply how many numbers you are willing to train.

Task 7 — Quantize harder

Modify the int8 routine to int4 (15 levels instead of 255). Measure the weight error and check whether the output survives.

Show Answer

Error rises sharply and a naive per-row int4 will usually damage a 0.5B model visibly. That is why real 4-bit schemes use finer groups, calibration data, and keep sensitive layers wider. Reproducing the failure is what makes the sophistication make sense.

Task 8 — Twenty examples

Build the twenty-example evaluation from the “choosing a model” section for a task you care about, and run three candidate models through it by hand.

Show Answer

Write the expected answers before you see any model output, or you will grade generously. Twenty examples is enough to eliminate a bad candidate and far more relevant than any public leaderboard.

Task 9 — Read a model card

Pick an open-weight model and answer from its card alone: parameters, context window, licence, languages claimed, and whether you may use it commercially.

Show Answer

Some of these are usually missing or vague, which is itself the finding. Note specifically whether Kazakh is listed, and whether a listed language is backed by any evaluation.

Task 10 — The whole course in one paragraph

Write the paragraph you would give a manager choosing between TF-IDF (Lecture 3), a fine-tuned encoder (Lecture 7), and an LLM API, for Kazakh sentiment classification.

Show Answer

You have measured numbers for the first two: 0.780 and 0.799 on the same test set. The honest paragraph gives accuracy, cost per call, latency, and where the data goes — and it is entirely possible that the correct recommendation is the cheapest of the three.

Summary

Today we learned

  • An LLM is Lecture 6’s decoder, scaled, trained in three stages instead of one
  • Pretraining buys knowledge; instruction tuning buys obedience; preference tuning buys judgement
  • fp16 needs about 2 GB per billion parameters — and training needs 6 to 8 times that
  • The KV cache can exceed the model: 16.8 GB for one 128k-token conversation on an 8B model
  • Open-weight is not open-source, and the licence is the part that matters
  • Billing is per token, output costs ~5× input, and the whole history is resent every turn
  • The same message costs 2.0× more tokens in Kazakh than English on o200k, 4.5× on cl100k
  • Choose by data residency and volume first; a twenty-example eval beats any leaderboard
  • Temperature reshapes the distribution; top-k and top-p truncate it; greedy is reproducible
  • Sampling cannot fix a wrong answer — greedy decoding invented a Caspian coast for Almaty
  • Reasoning models buy accuracy with test-time tokens, and are wasted on transformation tasks
  • RLHF turns “B is better than A” into a better model; DPO does it without a reward model
  • LoRA trains 0.219% of the parameters at rank 8 and ships a 4 MB adapter
  • int8 is 4× smaller for ~1% weight error and, here, an identical output
  • Fine-tune last: prompt, then examples, then retrieval, then fine-tuning
