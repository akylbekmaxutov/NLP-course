Lecture 11

Speech: ASR and TTS

Every lecture so far started with text that someone had already typed. Most language is not typed — it is spoken. This lecture closes the loop at both ends: sound into text with Whisper, text into sound with OmniVoice, and an honest way to score each of them on Kazakh.

Learning goals

By the end of this lecture you can…

Explain how audio becomes input

Sampling, the spectrogram, and why 16 000 numbers a second have to become about 50 frames a second before a Transformer can read them.

Describe Whisper

Lecture 6’s encoder–decoder with audio on the front, and the special tokens that decide what language it writes in.

Measure a recogniser

Word error rate and character error rate, why one can exceed 1.0, and why the gap between them is where the interesting finding lives.

Synthesise speech

Text to audio tokens to a waveform, and the three ways to choose whose voice comes out.

Speak it, transcribe it back, and read the number against a control — because on its own it measures two systems at once.

Both models run on the laptop. openai/whisper-small (244 M) and k2-fsa/OmniVoice (Qwen3-0.6 B plus an audio tokenizer), on an Apple M4 Pro, no API key. The audio is FLEURS: the same source sentences read aloud in every language it covers, which is what makes the comparisons on this page fair.

Theory · The input

What a computer receives when someone speaks

A microphone measures air pressure, over and over. At 16 kHz that is sixteen thousand numbers per second — and they are the wrong numbers, in the wrong quantity, for a Transformer.

Three ideas you need before the model
sampling rate
16 kHz is the speech standard. It captures frequencies up to 8 kHz, which is enough for every speech sound and half the size of music-quality audio. Every clip on this page was resampled to it.
the spectrogram
Cut the signal into overlapping 25 ms windows every 10 ms, and for each window measure how much energy sits in each of 80 frequency bands, spaced the way human hearing spaces them. The result is a picture: time across, frequency up.
it is now a sequence
80 numbers per frame, 100 frames per second. That is exactly the shape Lecture 6’s encoder expects — a sequence of vectors — which is why the same architecture works on sound with no change to the attention at all.

Nothing in the Transformer knows this is audio. Swap the embedding table for a spectrogram and the rest of Lecture 6 runs unmodified.

Theory · The model

Whisper is Lecture 6 with a microphone

There is no new architecture here. An encoder reads the spectrogram, a decoder writes text, and cross-attention connects them — exactly the encoder–decoder Transformer from Lecture 6, with audio replacing the source sentence.

The tokens before the text

The decoder does not start empty. It is forced to begin with a short prefix of special tokens, and the second one names the language. 

from transformers import WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration

processor = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-small")
model = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-small")

features = processor(samples, sampling_rate=16000,
                     return_tensors="pt").input_features

ids = model.generate(features, language="kk", task="transcribe")
print(processor.batch_decode(ids, skip_special_tokens=True)[0])
Theory · Inside the encoder

What the audio encoder actually does

“An encoder reads the spectrogram” is true and not very useful. Here is every step, with the real tensor shapes printed out of whisper-small. 

features = processor(samples, sampling_rate=16000,
                     return_tensors="pt").input_features
encoder = model.model.encoder

print(features.shape)                 # (1, 80, 3000)
print(encoder.conv1, encoder.conv2)   # 80->768 stride 1, 768->768 stride 2
print(encoder(features).last_hidden_state.shape)   # (1, 1500, 768)
Five things that surprise people
the input is always 30 seconds
A 3-second clip is padded with silence to 30 and the encoder runs on all of it. The shape is fixed, so a short clip costs the same as a long one. This is why batching short utterances is wasteful, and why long audio has to be cut into 30-second chunks.
the convolutions are the whole front end
Two 1-D convolutions over time, kernel 3. The first turns 80 mel bins into 768 channels; the second has stride 2 and is the only place the sequence gets shorter. 3 000 frames become 1 500.
50 frames a second is the working resolution
One vector per 20 ms. Speech sounds last 50–150 ms, so every phoneme spans several frames — enough resolution to distinguish them, few enough that attention over 1 500 positions is affordable.
positions are sinusoidal and frozen
Not learned, unlike the decoder’s. Lecture 6 introduced both kinds; here the fixed variety is used because the input length never varies.
no causal mask
The encoder sees the entire 30 seconds at once, so a word at the end can disambiguate a word at the start. That is precisely what streaming recognition gives up, and why real-time systems are worse than offline ones on the same audio.

The decoder is the bigger half — 153.6 M against 88.2 M. Most of Whisper is not listening; it is writing.

Theory · Measurement

How to score a transcript

Two numbers, both of them edit distances, one counted in words and one in characters. The difference between them turns out to be the most informative thing on this page.

WER = (substitutions + deletions + insertions) ÷ words in the reference. CER is the same arithmetic over characters. Both are computed after lowercasing and stripping punctuation — otherwise you are mostly measuring whether the model felt like writing a comma.

Why both numbers, and not just WER
WER is what a user feels
A wrong word is a wrong word, whether it missed by one letter or ten. For search, subtitles or a voice command, this is the number that matters.
CER is what the model heard
If CER is low while WER is high, the model got the sounds nearly right and the spelling wrong. That is a very different failure from not understanding the audio at all.
the gap is diagnostic
Measured below: Kazakh WER is 7.5× English, but Kazakh CER is only 4.3×. Whisper is hearing Kazakh tolerably and writing it badly — a tokenizer and training-data problem, not a deafness problem.
normalise first, and say how
Ours lowercases, removes punctuation and collapses whitespace. Published WER figures are not comparable across papers unless the normalisation matches, and it usually does not.
Theory · The algorithm

How WER is actually computed

The definition says “substitutions plus deletions plus insertions”, which raises the real question: who decides which words were substituted and which were deleted? The answer is an alignment, found by dynamic programming, and it is the same Levenshtein edit distance you may have met on characters.

WER is the minimum number of word edits that turn the hypothesis into the reference, divided by the length of the reference. “Minimum” is doing the work: of the many ways to line up two sequences, the score uses the cheapest one.

Consequences of it being a minimum
the alignment is a guess, not ground truth
Nobody recorded which word the model “meant”. The algorithm picks the cheapest story consistent with the two strings, and when several stories cost the same it picks one arbitrarily. For counting errors that is fine; for blaming a particular word it is weaker than it looks.
CER is the same algorithm on characters
One function, two tokenisations. That is the entire difference, and it is why the two numbers can be compared at all.
it costs O(N×M)
Fine for sentences. For a two-hour transcript it is worth chunking, which is what every real evaluation toolkit does.
a word is whatever you say it is
We split on whitespace after stripping punctuation. In an agglutinative language that is a real choice: Kazakh кітаптарымыздан is one “word” here and five morphemes linguistically, so one wrong suffix costs a whole word. Lecture 2’s tokenisation problem, still unresolved, now deciding a metric.

Read the errors, not just the number
21 reference words, 13 substituted, 3 deleted, 0 inserted
(13 + 3 + 0) / 21 = 0.762. Five words came through untouched.
гимнастика → димнастика
One letter. Counted as a whole wrong word by WER, as one wrong character by CER. Multiply that across a transcript and you get the 7.5× against 4.3× gap.
ақш-тың → ақыштың
An abbreviation plus a case suffix, which is the hard case in an agglutinative language: Lecture 2’s morphology problem, arriving through a microphone.
0.6 s for 14.9 s of audio
A real-time factor of 0.04, about 25× faster than the speech itself, on a laptop with no GPU server. Recognition is cheap; being right is the expensive part.
Result · The main finding

The same model, three languages

FLEURS reads the same source sentences in every language it covers. So these three rows differ in language and in nothing else that matters. 

  language    clips      WER      CER    audio s      RTF
  --------------------------------------------------------
  en             12    0.099    0.042      107.1     0.02
  ru             12    0.108    0.028      138.9     0.02
  kk             12    0.744    0.180      187.6     0.03

  Kazakh WER is 7.5x the English WER on the same sentences.
What 0.744 actually means
three out of four words are wrong
As a subtitle track it is unusable. As a search index it is close to useless: the words you would search for are the rare ones, and the rare ones are what it gets wrong.
but it is not deaf
CER 0.180 means five of six characters are right. Read the transcript aloud and a Kazakh speaker recognises the sentence. The model has the sound and not the spelling.
this is a data problem, not a language problem
Whisper was trained on 680 000 hours scraped from the web, in which Kazakh is a rounding error. Nothing about Kazakh is intrinsically harder to hear.
you have seen this number before
Lecture 7: 0.937 on English sentiment against 0.799 on Kazakh. Lecture 8: the same sentence costs 2.0× the tokens in Kazakh. Lecture 10: the geocoder could not find «Алматы». The penalty follows the language through every layer of the stack.

For a Kazakhstan product this is the number that decides the architecture. At WER 0.744 you do not build voice search; you build a fallback, or you fine-tune, or you pay for a bigger model.

Result · Size

Does a bigger model fix it?

  3. MODEL SIZE AGAINST ACCURACY  (kk)

  model      parameters      WER      CER      RTF
  ------------------------------------------------
  tiny             39 M    2.099    0.894     0.02
  base             74 M    0.934    0.322     0.02
  small           244 M    0.744    0.180     0.03

WER 2.099 is not a typo. tiny produces more than twice as many errors as there are words in the reference, because it inserts text that was never spoken — the decoder is a language model, and a small language model with a weak audio signal starts writing fiction. This is the clearest possible demonstration that WER is errors per word, not a percentage correct.

Reading the ladder
6× the parameters buys a lot, then less
39 M to 74 M nearly halves CER (0.894 to 0.322). 74 M to 244 M halves it again (0.322 to 0.180) for 3.3× the parameters. The returns are real and diminishing.
speed barely changes
RTF 0.02 to 0.03 across a 6× range in size. On this task the models are all far faster than real time, so size costs memory, not waiting.
0.744 is still not usable
Scaling within the family did not solve Kazakh. The honest options are a larger checkpoint (large-v3), fine-tuning on Kazakh speech, or designing the product so it never needs a perfect transcript.
Result · A trap

Naming the wrong language

The same Kazakh clip, decoded four times, changing only the language token. 

  reference : ақш-тың гимнастика федерациясы ақш олимпиада комитетінің хатын қолдайды…

  language=kk  WER 0.762  Ақыштың димнастика федерациясы Ақыш Олимпиады қамитетінің…
  language=ru  WER 0.905  Акшн димнастика Федерация Акш Олимпиада комитетов, хану…
  language=tr  WER 1.095  Akıştın Dimyastika Fidiratiası Akış Aleyküya'da komite…
  language=en  WER 1.095  The Dymnastic Federation was the chairman of the Olympic…

Look at the last line. Told the audio is English, Whisper produces a grammatical English sentence about an Olympic committee chairman. It is fluent, it is confident, and it is invented — the decoder’s language model filling in over an audio signal it cannot map to English words.

Two practical consequences
never hard-code the language in a multilingual product
In Kazakhstan a support line receives Kazakh and Russian in the same queue, often in the same sentence. A fixed language="ru" silently turns every Kazakh call into transliterated noise, and nothing in the output signals it.
detect it, and look at the confidence
Whisper can predict the language token itself rather than being forced. That is a real classifier with a probability on it; log the probability and route low-confidence audio to a human.

Transcribe against translate

  task=transcribe  0.5 s
    Ақыштың димнастика федерациясы Ақыш Олимпиады қамитетінің ханғылдайды, …

  task=translate   0.2 s
    The Dymnastic Federation of Akhars is the leader of the Akhars Olympic
    Committee. It is the center of the community of our sportsmen, athletes,
    and athletes.

One model, one encoder pass, a different third token. The English is grammatical and substantively wrong — “Federation of Akhars”, “athletes, and athletes” — because translation inherits every recognition error and then smooths it into fluent prose. If Kazakh recognition is at WER 0.744, Kazakh-to-English translation cannot be better than that, and it will look much better than it is.

Theory · Synthesis

The same problem, backwards

Recognition compresses 16 000 numbers a second down to three words. Synthesis has to expand three words a second back into 16 000 numbers — and unlike recognition, there is no single right answer. Every sentence has infinitely many correct readings, differing in voice, pace and emphasis. 

from omnivoice import OmniVoice

model = OmniVoice.from_pretrained("k2-fsa/OmniVoice", device_map="mps")

audio = model.generate(
    text="Сәлеметсіз бе! Бүгін Астанада ауа райы жақсы.",
    language="Kazakh",
)                      # a list of numpy arrays at 24 kHz

import soundfile as sf
sf.write("out.wav", audio[0], 24000)

Three ways to choose the voice

auto

Say nothing and the model picks a voice. Fast — 2.96 s of audio in 1.9 s — and you get whatever it felt like. Fine for a prototype, useless for a product with a brand.

voice design

Describe the speaker with attributes: female, young adult, high pitch. No reference audio needed at all. Same speed as auto.

voice clone

Give it a few seconds of a real speaker and a transcript of that audio, and it copies the voice. Slower — 11.6 s here, because the reference has to be encoded too.

Voice design takes a controlled vocabulary, not prose. “A calm, warm female voice speaking slowly and clearly” is rejected, with an error listing the 23 attributes it will accept: female, male, child, teenager, young adult, middle-aged, elderly, five pitch levels, whisper, and ten accents. This is exactly Lecture 10’s JSON finding in a different costume: getting the shape right is not the same as getting the vocabulary right, and a good API tells you which words exist.

Theory · Inside the synthesiser

How a model actually makes a voice

A model cannot predict 24 000 numbers a second one at a time — nothing could. The trick that makes modern TTS work is to invent a vocabulary for sound, so that generating speech becomes the same problem as generating text.

Step 1 — give sound an alphabet

A neural audio codec is an autoencoder whose middle is forced to be discrete. It compresses a waveform into a short sequence of integers and reconstructs the waveform from them. OmniVoice uses one called Higgs Audio v2, and we can just ask it what it does: 

tokenizer = model.audio_tokenizer          # HiggsAudioV2TokenizerModel
codes = tokenizer.encode(torch.zeros(1, 1, 24000 * 2))   # 2 seconds
print(codes.shape)                          # (1, 8, 50)
print(tokenizer.decode(codes).shape)        # (1, 1, 48000) -> 2.00 s at 24 kHz
  1 s -> codes (1, 8, 25)     25.0 frames/s   200 codes total (8 codebooks)
  2 s -> codes (1, 8, 50)     25.0 frames/s   400 codes total (8 codebooks)
  4 s -> codes (1, 8, 100)    25.0 frames/s   800 codes total (8 codebooks)
  8 s -> codes (1, 8, 200)    25.0 frames/s  1600 codes total (8 codebooks)

Step 2 — predict those integers from text

Now the job is familiar: given text, produce a sequence of codes. OmniVoice’s backbone is Qwen3 — 28 layers, the same architecture family as Lecture 8’s language models — with the audio vocabulary bolted on beside the text one.

One twist: it is a diffusion language model, not a left-to-right one. The audio vocabulary has 1 025 entries, and the extra one is a <mask>. Generation starts with every audio position masked and unmasks them over several passes, refining the whole utterance at once rather than committing to the first 40 ms before it has decided how the sentence ends. That is why prosody comes out coherent, and why the real-time factor (0.62) is worse than a recogniser’s (0.03) — several passes over the sequence instead of one.

Step 3 — so what is a voice?

Not a setting

There is no “speaker” parameter and no stored library of people. The identity lives entirely in the conditioning you put in front of the text.

It is context

For cloning, the reference clip is encoded into the same audio codes, and those codes are placed in the context. The model then continues in a way that is consistent with them — the same in-context learning as Lecture 9’s few-shot prompting, with audio tokens as the examples.

Which explains the three modes, and their costs
auto — no conditioning
Nothing constrains the voice, so the model samples one consistent with the language and the text. Cheapest: 2.96 s of audio in 1.9 s.
voice design — conditioning by label
female, elderly, low pitch are special tokens the model was trained alongside, which is exactly why free prose is rejected: an unseen phrase is not a token it has ever been conditioned on. The vocabulary is the interface.
voice clone — conditioning by example
The reference is encoded to codes and prepended, so a 14.9-second reference is roughly 14.9 × 200 = 3 000 extra tokens of context. That is why cloning took 11.6 s against 1.9 s: you are paying to read the reference, every time.
and why the reference transcript is needed
The model is given the reference audio and the words it contains, so it can learn the mapping from those words to that speaker’s sounds. Give it a transcript that does not match and the mapping it infers is wrong everywhere — measured below as WER 0.667 becoming 1.000.
Result · Scoring a voice

The round trip

How do you score a synthesiser without a listening panel? Close the loop: speak the sentence, transcribe it back with Whisper, and measure how much survived. It is cheap, it is repeatable, and it is wrong in one specific way you have to control for. 

  2. ROUND TRIP: TEXT -> SPEECH -> TEXT

  language     lines      WER      CER      RTF    human WER
  ----------------------------------------------------------
  en               4    0.083    0.024     0.62        0.099
  ru               4    0.146    0.054     0.57        0.108
  kk               4    0.787    0.240     0.62        0.744
What this decomposition shows
English round-trip 0.083 — better than human speech
Whisper makes fewer mistakes on OmniVoice’s English than on the human FLEURS readers (0.099). Synthetic speech is clean, evenly paced and free of the room noise and hesitation in real recordings. The synthesiser is genuinely good.
so the Kazakh 0.787 is not the voice’s fault
It sits barely above Whisper’s 0.744 on human Kazakh. Almost the entire error is the recogniser’s, measured in the previous section. The same pipeline on English proves the synthesis half works.
which is why the control column exists
Report 0.787 alone and a reader concludes the Kazakh voice is broken. That conclusion would be wrong, and only the English row rules it out. A metric that measures two systems at once needs a condition where one of them is known good.
real-time factor about 0.6
Synthesis is roughly 20× slower than recognition and still faster than speech. A sentence takes about 60% of the time it takes to say it, on a laptop.

Note what this does not measure: whether the voice sounds natural, pleasant, or correctly stressed. A robotic monotone can score perfectly. The round trip measures intelligibility and nothing else — useful, cheap, and not a substitute for listening.

Result · Pipelines

Errors travel

Cloning a voice needs the reference audio and a transcript of it. In a language your recogniser is weak at, that transcript is itself a guess — so the two systems on this page feed each other their mistakes. 

  reference clip : kk-00.wav (14.9 s)
  true transcript: ақш-тың гимнастика федерациясы ақш олимпиада комитетінің…
  whisper heard  : Ақыштың димнастика федерациясы Ақыш Олимпиады қамитетінің…

  Now make that speaker say something new:
  «Алматыдан Шымкентке дейінгі жол тоғыз сағат.»

  ref_text = true transcript     ->  round-trip WER 0.667   CER 0.209
  ref_text = whisper transcript  ->  round-trip WER 1.000   CER 0.279

0.667 becomes 1.000. Same audio, same target sentence, same models. The only change is whether the reference transcript was correct or was Whisper’s best effort at it. Every word in the output was wrong.

The general lesson, which is not about speech
a pipeline multiplies its weakest stage
Two components at 75% each are not a 75% system. Lecture 10 made this point about agents chaining tool calls; here it is two models, and the number is stark.
the second stage cannot tell it was given rubbish
OmniVoice has no way to know the reference text does not match the reference audio. It produced confident, fluent speech from an inconsistent pair.
so measure end to end, not stage by stage
Whisper at 0.744 and OmniVoice at “works fine in English” both look acceptable in isolation. Only the joint measurement shows a complete failure.
and prefer human transcripts where you can
For a product that clones a speaker, record the reference script rather than transcribing whatever you were given. It is the cheapest fix on this page.
Theory · Evaluation

Evaluating a synthesiser properly

Our round trip measures one thing well and three things not at all. A synthesiser is judged on four separate axes, they do not correlate, and a system can be excellent on one while failing on another.

Four axes of TTS evaluation
What you want to know How it is measured Needs people? Did we?
Intelligibility
can the words be recovered		 WER/CER of an ASR transcript of the synthetic audio no yes — 0.083 en / 0.787 kk
Naturalness
does it sound like a person		 MOS: listeners rate 1–5; or CMOS, A/B against a reference yes no
Speaker similarity
is it the right person		 cosine similarity between speaker embeddings of reference and output no no
Prosody
right rhythm, stress, intonation		 duration and pitch statistics against a human reading; or listeners partly no
How each one is really done
MOS, and why it is harder than it sounds
Listeners rate each clip 1 to 5 and you average. Scores are not comparable between studies — different raters, instructions and reference clips move the mean by more than most model improvements — so a MOS of 4.1 in one paper and 3.9 in another says nothing. Within a single experiment, with the same raters hearing both systems, it is meaningful.
CMOS, which is what teams actually use
Play A and B and ask which is better, on a scale from −3 to +3. Comparative judgements are far more stable than absolute ones, and it answers the question you actually have: is the new checkpoint better than the old one?
speaker similarity
Run reference and output through a speaker-verification model — the same kind used to tell whether two recordings are the same person — and take the cosine between the embeddings. This is the number to watch for cloning, and we did not measure it: our cloning section scored intelligibility, which can stay high while the voice drifts to someone else entirely.
prosody
Compare the synthetic utterance with a human reading of the same sentence: total duration, pause placement, pitch contour. Useful as a regression check, weak as a quality measure, because there are many acceptable readings of any sentence — exactly the ambiguity that makes TTS harder to score than ASR.

For a Kazakh product, one more axis matters and none of the four covers it: whether the pronunciation is correct Kazakh rather than merely intelligible. That needs a native speaker, and no amount of automatic scoring replaces the ten minutes it takes to ask one.

What we would add next, in order. First speaker-similarity cosine, because it is automatic and it is the thing our cloning experiment is blind to. Then a CMOS test with a handful of Kazakh speakers comparing OmniVoice against a human recording — twenty clips and an afternoon. Everything else is refinement.

What the pattern means for building things here
the penalty compounds down a pipeline
Speech at 0.744, then a tokenizer charging double, then a model trained mostly on other languages. Each stage is survivable; stacked, they are not. Build with the fewest stages you can.

OmniVoice covers 651 languages deliberately, and its Kazakh is good enough that Whisper is the bottleneck. Synthesis got multilingual before recognition did.

In class

Knowledge check

Eight questions we answer together before finishing.

Question 1

Why does audio have to become a spectrogram before a Transformer sees it?

Show Answer

Length, mostly. Attention is quadratic in sequence length, and 30 seconds at 16 kHz is 480 000 samples. The spectrogram turns that into 3 000 frames of 80 numbers, and the encoder’s stride-2 convolution halves it again to 1 500 — 320× fewer time steps — while keeping the frequency structure that distinguishes speech sounds. After that it is just a sequence of vectors, which is what Lecture 6’s encoder already accepted.

Question 2

Whisper scored WER 2.099 on Kazakh with the tiny model. How can an error rate be above 1?

Show Answer

Because WER counts insertions, and is divided by the length of the reference, not the hypothesis. A model that writes 60 words where 21 were spoken can commit more errors than there are reference words. The decoder is a language model; starved of a usable audio signal it starts producing fluent text of its own.

Question 3

Kazakh WER is 7.5× English but Kazakh CER is only 4.3×. What does that gap tell you?

Show Answer

That the model is hearing roughly the right sounds and writing the wrong words. гимнастика came out as димнастика — one character wrong, one whole word wrong. If CER were also catastrophic the model would not be resolving Kazakh phonetics at all, and the fix would be different. The gap points at training data and spelling, not at the acoustics.

Question 4

A colleague hard-codes language="ru" because most of their callers speak Russian. What happens to the Kazakh callers?

Show Answer

They get fluent transliterated nonsense, with nothing in the output to indicate a problem — WER 0.905 instead of 0.762 in our measurement, and worse, it looks like Russian. The language token is the token the decoder is forced to start from, not a hint it can override. Let Whisper predict the language and log the probability.

Question 5

The round-trip WER for Kazakh was 0.787. Is the Kazakh voice bad?

Show Answer

No, and you cannot tell from that number alone — which is the lesson. Whisper scores 0.744 on human Kazakh, so almost all of 0.787 is the recogniser. The English control settles it: round-trip English is 0.083, better than Whisper on human English (0.099). The synthesiser works; the recogniser is the bottleneck.

Question 6

Round-trip WER for English was lower than Whisper's error on human English. Should that worry you?

Show Answer

It should make you careful about what the metric means. Synthetic speech is clean, evenly paced, noise-free and has no hesitation or accent, so it is genuinely easier to recognise than a real recording. The round trip measures intelligibility to a machine — not naturalness, not pleasantness, not correct stress. A flat robotic monotone could score perfectly.

Question 7

Cloning with the true transcript scored 0.667; cloning with Whisper's transcript of the same clip scored 1.000. Why so severe?

Show Answer

The cloner aligns the reference audio with the reference text to work out how this speaker realises each sound. Give it a text that does not match the audio and that alignment is wrong everywhere, so the voice it extracts is wrong everywhere. It cannot detect the inconsistency, and it produces confident, fluent, unusable speech. Two components that each look acceptable alone made a system that fails completely.

Question 8

You are asked to build Kazakh voice search for a Kazakhstan app. What do you say?

Show Answer

That at WER 0.744 with whisper-small it will not work as specified, and here is the measurement. Then the options: a larger checkpoint, fine-tuning on Kazakh speech corpora, restricting to a small command vocabulary where a constrained decoder can be right far more often, or falling back to typing when confidence is low. The useful contribution is the number, taken on your own language, before anyone has built anything.

Summary

Today we learned

  • Speech reaches a model as a spectrogram: 80 mel bands, 50 frames a second, which is just a sequence of vectors
  • Whisper is Lecture 6’s encoder–decoder with audio on the front; nothing about the attention changed
  • Its decoder is a language model, which is why its mistakes are fluent real words rather than noise
  • The language token is the decoder’s starting point, not a hint — name the wrong one and you get confident transliteration
  • WER counts substitutions, deletions and insertions per reference word, so it can exceed 1.0; whisper-tiny scored 2.099 on Kazakh
  • On the same FLEURS sentences: English WER 0.099, Russian 0.108, Kazakh 0.744 — 7.5× worse
  • But Kazakh CER is only 4.3× worse: the model hears the sounds and writes the wrong words
  • Scaling tiny → base → small took Kazakh CER 0.894 → 0.322 → 0.180, and still did not reach usable
  • Recognition runs at RTF 0.02–0.03 on a laptop; being right is the expensive part, not being fast
  • Synthesis is the same problem backwards, with no single right answer — text becomes audio tokens, then a waveform
  • OmniVoice picks a voice three ways: auto, voice design from a fixed vocabulary, or cloning from a reference clip
  • Voice design rejects free prose and lists its 23 allowed attributes — structure is not vocabulary, as in Lecture 10
  • Score a synthesiser by speaking and transcribing back — but only against a control, or the number blames two systems at once
  • Round-trip English scored 0.083, better than Whisper on human English, so the synthesiser is good and Kazakh’s 0.787 is the recogniser’s doing
  • Feeding Whisper’s own transcript into the voice cloner took it from WER 0.667 to 1.000 — errors propagate and compound
  • The Kazakh penalty has now been measured at five layers of this course, and it compounds down a pipeline
