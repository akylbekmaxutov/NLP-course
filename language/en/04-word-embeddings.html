Theory · The core idea

What an embedding is

A model cannot read. Everything it does is arithmetic, so a word has to become numbers before anything else can happen. Lectures 2 and 3 turned words into numbers by counting them. This lecture turns them into numbers by placing them.

Step 1 — a word becomes a list of numbers

Two numbers place a point on a sheet of paper. Three place it in a room. One hundred place it in a space nobody can picture — but the arithmetic for “how far apart are these two points” works exactly the same in all three cases. That is the whole trick: give every word a fixed-length list of numbers, and questions about meaning turn into questions about position.

An embedding is a list of numbers attached to a word — its coordinates. Every word gets a list of the same length (100 numbers in this lecture), and the lists are chosen so that words used in similar ways end up in similar places.

To see what that means, here is a tiny table written by hand, where we picked the two dimensions ourselves so they can be read: how positive the word is and how much it is about connectivity. 

import numpy as np

# A toy embedding table, written by hand so we can read it.
# Two dimensions we chose ourselves: "how positive" and "how much about internet".
words = {
    "жақсы":    np.array([0.9, 0.1]),   # positive, not about internet
    "керемет":  np.array([0.95, 0.05]),
    "нашар":    np.array([-0.9, 0.1]),  # negative
    "интернет": np.array([0.0, 0.95]),  # neutral, all about internet
    "байланыс": np.array([0.1, 0.9]),
}

def cosine(a, b):
    return float(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))

print("every word is a point:")
for word, vector in words.items():
    print(f"  {word:9} {vector}")

print("\ncloseness = cosine of the angle between two points:")
for a, b in [("жақсы", "керемет"), ("жақсы", "нашар"),
             ("интернет", "байланыс"), ("жақсы", "интернет")]:
    print(f"  {a:9} ~ {b:9} {cosine(words[a], words[b]):+.2f}")

Step 2 — closeness is an angle

Once words are points, “do these two words mean similar things?” becomes “do these two points lie in the same direction?” The measure is the cosine similarity from Lecture 2 — the same formula, applied to word vectors instead of document vectors.

How to read a cosine
+1
the same direction — used in the same way
0
at right angles — nothing in common
-1
opposite directions

Why the angle and not the distance: the length of a vector says little about meaning, so we compare which way the words point rather than how far out they sit.

In the hand-made table, жақсы ~ керемет gives +1.00 and жақсы ~ нашар gives −0.98, because we placed them there on purpose. The interesting question is whether numbers found automatically, by a program that was never told what any word means, do the same thing.

Step 3 — the real vectors, and what they look like

These are the vectors trained later on this page, on 134 368 Kazakh app reviews. Nothing about them was written by hand. 

import numpy as np
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

word2vec = Word2Vec.load("models/word2vec.model")

Three things to take from this output. First, a word is now 100 numbers, and none of them is readable on its own. Second, the cosine really is just that one line of arithmetic — computing it by hand and asking gensim give the same 0.726. Third, look at dimension 7: its top-scoring words are менің, ішінде, емес, соны, шығар — no theme at all. The readable axes of the hand-made table were a teaching device. In a real embedding no single dimension means anything; only the positions of words relative to each other do.

Step 4 — where the numbers come from

Nobody types them in, and there is no dictionary involved. Every word starts with 100 random numbers — a meaningless space — and those numbers are then corrected millions of times:

  1. Give every word in the vocabulary 100 random numbers.
  2. Slide a small window along the text and take a pair: a word and one of the words actually standing next to it.
  3. Ask the current numbers a question — judging by these coordinates, does this pair look like a real neighbour pair?
  4. The answer is usually wrong, so nudge the two vectors a little towards each other. Take a few pairs that never occur together and nudge those apart.
  5. Repeat for every position in the corpus, ten times over.

No one labelled anything. The text supplies both the question (a word) and the answer (its real neighbours), which is why plain, unlabelled Kazakh text is enough — and why the millions of unlabelled sentences on the internet suddenly become useful.

Words that keep appearing next to the same neighbours get nudged towards the same region over and over, so they end up close together. That is the entire mechanism, and the rest of this lecture is its detail: which pairs are chosen, how the nudge is computed, what breaks for a language like Kazakh, and whether any of it improves the classifier from Lecture 3.

Theory · The problem

What TF-IDF cannot do

Three limitations followed us out of Lecture 2, and Lecture 3 measured the cost of all of them.

Synonyms are strangers

жақсы and керемет are two unrelated columns. A review that says one gives the model nothing about the other.

Unseen words vanish

A word missing from the training vocabulary is simply dropped. In Kazakh, where 69% of the vocabulary appeared exactly once, that happens constantly.

Vectors are huge and empty

9 419 features for 8 000 short reviews, almost all zeros. Most of the matrix carries no information at all.

Nothing transfers

Everything the model knows comes from labelled data. The millions of unlabelled Kazakh sentences on the internet are wasted.

Embeddings attack all four. They are learned from raw text with no labels, they are dense and small, they give related words related vectors, and — with fastText — they can build a vector for a word they have never seen.

Theory · The idea

The distributional hypothesis

You shall know a word by the company it keeps. Words that appear in similar contexts tend to mean similar things — so if we can describe a word by its neighbours, we have described its meaning.

The same slot, two words 
Қосымша өте ______ екен, рахмет!
                жақсы
                керемет

Both words fit. Across a whole corpus, жақсы and керемет keep turning up in the same slots — next to өте, next to рахмет, in reviews with five stars. Nothing else needs to be known about them for a model to notice they belong together.

A schematic view of 100 dimensions squeezed into two. The cosine values are the real ones, measured later on this page.

Theory · The model

word2vec

word2vec (Mikolov and colleagues, 2013) turns the distributional hypothesis into a prediction task. The vectors are not the goal of the training — they are what is left behind once the task is learned.

Two ways round the same idea

Skip-gram

Given the centre word, predict the words around it. Slower, and better on small corpora and rare words — which is why we use it here.

CBOW

Given the surrounding words, predict the centre one. Faster, and smoother on frequent words. In gensim: sg=0.

Skip-gram with a window of two: from жақсы, predict the four words around it. Every position in the corpus becomes a training example.

How it actually trains

Predicting one word out of a 27 000-word vocabulary would need a 27 000-way softmax at every step, which is far too slow. Negative sampling replaces it with a much cheaper question: is this pair of words a real neighbour pair, or one I invented?

  • (жақсы, өте)→
  • real — push the vectors together
  • (жақсы, банк)→
  • invented — push them apart

A handful of invented pairs per real pair is enough. The vectors start random and are nudged, pair by pair, until words that share company sit near each other. Nobody ever tells the model what жақсы means.

What the parameters mean. vector_size — how many numbers per word; 100–300 is normal. window — how far “context” reaches; small windows capture syntax, large ones capture topic. min_count — ignore words rarer than this. epochs — how many passes over the corpus; small corpora need more.
Practice · The corpus

Preparing the corpus

Training needs one thing: a list of sentences, each already split into words. No labels, no annotation, no cleaning beyond lowercasing.

We use the KazSAnDRA reviews from Lecture 3, but this time we take all of them and ignore the label column entirely. Lecture 3 could only use the 8 000 reviews of its balanced training split, because a classifier needs answers to learn from. An embedding does not, so all 134 368 are useful here. 

import os
import re

from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv(".env")                      # the file holds HF_TOKEN=hf_...

from datasets import load_dataset

reviews = load_dataset("issai/kazsandra", "polarity_classification",
                       split="train", token=os.getenv("HF_TOKEN")).to_pandas()

TOKEN = re.compile(r"\w+")

def tokenize(text):
    return TOKEN.findall(str(text).lower())

corpus = [tokenize(text) for text in reviews["text_cleaned"].astype(str)]

print("reviews  :", len(corpus))
print("tokens   :", sum(len(sentence) for sentence in corpus))
print("longest  :", max(len(sentence) for sentence in corpus), "tokens")
print("distinct :", len(set(word for sentence in corpus for word in sentence)), "words")
print("example  :", corpus[0])

Why the tokenizer is this simple. \w+ keeps runs of letters and digits and throws away everything else. It is the same rule the classifier will use later, and that matters more than sophistication: if the embeddings are trained on one kind of token and looked up with another, most lookups miss.

Practice · Training

Training word2vec with gensim

gensim is the standard Python library for this. It takes the list of tokenized sentences and gives back a lookup table of vectors. 

import os
import re

from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv(".env")

from datasets import load_dataset
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

reviews = load_dataset("issai/kazsandra", "polarity_classification",
                       split="train", token=os.getenv("HF_TOKEN")).to_pandas()
corpus = [re.findall(r"\w+", str(text).lower())
          for text in reviews["text_cleaned"].astype(str)]

word2vec = Word2Vec(
    corpus,
    vector_size=100,   # numbers per word
    window=5,          # words of context on each side
    min_count=3,       # ignore words seen fewer than three times
    sg=1,              # 1 = skip-gram, 0 = CBOW
    epochs=10,         # passes over the corpus
    workers=1,         # one thread, so two runs give the same vectors
    seed=42,
)

os.makedirs("models", exist_ok=True)
word2vec.save("models/word2vec.model")

print("vocabulary :", len(word2vec.wv), "words")
print("per word   :", word2vec.wv.vector_size, "numbers")
print("dropped    :", len(set(w for s in corpus for w in s)) - len(word2vec.wv),
      "words seen fewer than three times")
print("saved to   : models/word2vec.model")
vocabulary : 27076 words
per word   : 100 numbers
dropped    : 92695 words seen fewer than three times
saved to   : models/word2vec.model
Every parameter, and what it changes
vector_size=100
numbers per word. More capacity, but also more text needed to fill it
window=5
how many words of context count as “nearby”. Small windows learn grammatical roles, large ones learn topics
min_count=3
skip words seen fewer than three times — here that is 92 695 of the 119 771 word types
sg=1
skip-gram. sg=0 would be CBOW: faster, usually weaker on rare words
epochs=10
passes over the corpus. On a small corpus more passes help; on a huge one they cost hours
workers=1
one thread, so two runs give the same vectors
seed=42
the random start. Combined with workers=1 it makes the run repeatable

Result: 27 076 words × 100 numbers, trained in about half a minute, saved as a file you can load again.

About workers=1. With several threads gensim updates shared weights in a racy order, so two runs of identical code give different vectors — the neighbours of жақсы change between runs. A single worker plus a fixed seed makes the result repeatable. Here it costs about twenty seconds; on a large corpus you will want the threads back and will have to accept that your numbers move a little.

Saving is the point of this step. Training is the slow part and it only has to happen once. word2vec.save(...) writes the model to disk, and from now on every example on this page starts with Word2Vec.load("models/word2vec.model") — a second or two instead of half a minute. That is also how you would use embeddings in a real project: train once, load everywhere.

Measuring “close”

from gensim.models import Word2Vec

word2vec = Word2Vec.load("models/word2vec.model")

for a, b in (("жақсы", "керемет"), ("жақсы", "нашар"),
             ("интернет", "байланыс"), ("интернет", "тамақ")):
    print(f"{a:>9} ~ {b:<10} {word2vec.wv.similarity(a, b):.3f}")
    жақсы ~ керемет    0.726
    жақсы ~ нашар      0.346
 интернет ~ байланыс   0.520
 интернет ~ тамақ      0.224

Synonyms score 0.726, opposites 0.346, unrelated words 0.224. The gap between the pairs is what a classifier will be able to use. But note that opposites are not at zero: жақсы and нашар appear in very similar contexts — both follow өте, both describe the same product. The distributional hypothesis cannot separate a word from its antonym, and that is a known weakness, not a bug in our training.

Where it fails: analogies

from gensim.models import Word2Vec

word2vec = Word2Vec.load("models/word2vec.model")

for positive, negative in ((["әйел", "патша"], ["ер"]), (["жаман"], ["жақсы"])):
    found = word2vec.wv.most_similar(positive=positive, negative=negative, topn=3)
    print(f"{positive} - {negative} → {[w for w, _ in found]}")
['әйел', 'патша'] - ['ер'] → ['дж', 'базасы', 'бөлімшелер']
['жаман'] - ['жақсы'] → ['фуу', 'мал', 'тартпандар']

Nonsense, and that is the honest result. The famous “king − man + woman = queen” demonstrations come from models trained on billions of tokens. We have 1.17 million — enough for neighbours, nowhere near enough for arithmetic. If you show analogies in a talk, say what size of corpus produced them.

Theory · The upgrade

fastText and subwords

word2vec has one hard limit: a word it never saw has no vector at all. For an agglutinative language that is severe — every case and possessive suffix creates a new form.

fastText represents a word as the sum of its character n-grams. кітаптарымыздан is built from кіт, ітап, тары, дан and the rest — so a form that never appeared in training still gets a sensible vector, assembled from pieces that did.

With min_n=3, max_n=5, every substring of length 3 to 5 becomes a piece of the word.

Training it is the same call

Same corpus, same settings, two extra parameters — and a second file on disk next to the first. 

import os
import re

from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv(".env")

from datasets import load_dataset
from gensim.models import FastText
from gensim.models.fasttext import compute_ngrams

reviews = load_dataset("issai/kazsandra", "polarity_classification",
                       split="train", token=os.getenv("HF_TOKEN")).to_pandas()
corpus = [re.findall(r"\w+", str(text).lower())
          for text in reviews["text_cleaned"].astype(str)]

fasttext = FastText(
    corpus,
    vector_size=100, window=5, min_count=3, sg=1,   # the same as word2vec
    epochs=10, workers=1, seed=42,
    min_n=3,           # shortest character n-gram
    max_n=5,           # longest character n-gram
)

fasttext.save("models/fasttext.model")

print("vocabulary  :", len(fasttext.wv), "words")
print("n-gram slots:", fasttext.wv.bucket)
print("pieces of «кітап»:", compute_ngrams("кітап", 3, 5)[:6], "…")
print("saved to    : models/fasttext.model")

This is the Kazakh result of the lecture. All three words are unknown to word2vec. fastText places кітаптарымыздан next to кітаптардан and кітаптан — same root, different suffixes — and интернетсіздіктен next to интернетсіз. The morphology that split our features in Lecture 2 is now doing useful work, because the pieces are shared.

The price. Look again at the fastText neighbours in the previous section: жақсы → жжақсы, эақсы, дақсы. Character n-grams cannot tell a suffix from a typo, so on noisy review text fastText partly learns spelling instead of meaning. word2vec gives cleaner synonyms; fastText gives coverage. Which matters more depends on your data.
Theory · From words to documents

One vector for a whole review

The classifier needs one vector per review, not one per word. The simplest answer, and a surprisingly strong one, is to average them.

  • өте+
  • керемет+
  • қосымша→
  • mean = one 100-dim vector
import re

import numpy as np
from gensim.models import Word2Vec

word2vec = Word2Vec.load("models/word2vec.model")

review = "қосымша өте жақсы рахмет"
words = re.findall(r"\w+", review.lower())

vectors = [word2vec.wv[word] for word in words if word in word2vec.wv]
document = np.mean(vectors, axis=0)

print("review   :", review)
print("words    :", words)
print("each word:", vectors[0].shape, "→", len(vectors), "vectors")
print("document :", document.shape)
print("first few:", document[:5].round(3))

each word: (100,) → 4 vectors
document : (100,)
first few: [-0.4   -0.223 -0.024  0.463 -0.162]

Four words, four vectors of 100 numbers, one average of 100 numbers. The review is now a single point in the same space as the words, and a classifier can be trained on points.

Averaging throws the order away again. We are back to a bag of words, only with better words. жақсы емес and емес жақсы produce exactly the same document vector. The bigrams of Lecture 2 fixed that for counts; for embeddings the fix is a model that reads sequences, which is where the course goes next.

Better schemes exist — weighting by idf, SIF with common component removal, doc2vec — but on this data plain averaging was the strongest of the ones we tried. Weighting by idf actually lowered accuracy — 0.787 for word2vec and 0.789 for fastText, against 0.794 and 0.792 for the plain mean — because it plays down exactly the frequent sentiment words that carry the label.

Reusing the models in the Lecture 3 classifier

Everything up to here trained and saved vectors. Now we load them back and do what Lecture 3 did — with one thing changed, the features.

import os

# 1. the models trained earlier — nothing is retrained here
word2vec = Word2Vec.load("models/word2vec.model")
fasttext = FastText.load("models/fasttext.model")


# 2. the drop-in replacement for TfidfVectorizer
class MeanEmbedding(BaseEstimator, TransformerMixin):
    """Average the word vectors of a document."""

    def __init__(self, model=None):
        self.model = model

    def fit(self, x, y=None):
        return self                       # nothing to learn: the vectors exist

    def transform(self, texts):
        vectors = self.model.wv
        out = np.zeros((len(texts), vectors.vector_size), dtype=np.float32)
        self.skipped_ = 0
        for i, text in enumerate(texts):
            found = []
            for word in re.findall(r"\w+", str(text).lower()):
                try:
                    found.append(vectors[word])
                except KeyError:          # word2vec only: unknown word
                    self.skipped_ += 1
            if found:
                out[i] = np.mean(found, axis=0)
        return out


# 3. the balanced split of Lecture 3
def balance(df, per_class, seed=42):
    parts = [g.sample(per_class, random_state=seed) for _, g in df.groupby("label")]
    return pd.concat(parts).sample(frac=1, random_state=seed).reset_index(drop=True)

def split(name, per_class):
    df = load_dataset("issai/kazsandra", "polarity_classification",
                      split=name, token=os.getenv("HF_TOKEN")).to_pandas()
    df = balance(df, per_class)
    return df["text_cleaned"].astype(str), df["label"]

x_train, y_train = split("train", 4000)
x_test, y_test = split("test", 1000)

# 4. the same classifier, three kinds of features
classifier = lambda: LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=5.0)
models = {
    "TF-IDF": Pipeline([("features", TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=2,
                                                     sublinear_tf=True)),
                        ("clf", classifier())]),
    "word2vec": Pipeline([("features", MeanEmbedding(word2vec)), ("clf", classifier())]),
    "fastText": Pipeline([("features", MeanEmbedding(fasttext)), ("clf", classifier())]),
}

predictions = {}
for name, model in models.items():
    model.fit(x_train, y_train)
    predictions[name] = model.predict(x_test)
    scores = cross_val_score(model, x_train, y_train, cv=5)
    skipped = getattr(model.named_steps["features"], "skipped_", 0)
    print(f"{name:<10} test={model.score(x_test, y_test):.3f}"
          f"   CV={scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}"
          f"   skipped {skipped} unknown tokens")

print()
print(classification_report(y_test, predictions["fastText"],
                            target_names=["negative", "positive"], digits=3))
print("confusion matrix (rows = true, columns = predicted)")
print(confusion_matrix(y_test, predictions["fastText"]))

names = {0: "negative", 1: "positive"}

fixed = [(t, y) for t, y, a, b in rows if a != y and b == y]
broken = [(t, y) for t, y, a, b in rows if a == y and b != y]

print(f"\nfastText fixed {len(fixed)} reviews that TF-IDF got wrong")
print(f"fastText broke {len(broken)} reviews that TF-IDF got right")
print(f"net change on {len(rows)} reviews: {len(fixed) - len(broken):+d}")
for text, label in fixed[:2]:

for text, label in broken[:2]:

two lines, and the models trained earlier are back. No retraining happens in this script at all

Did it help?

Yes, by about 0.014. Both embedding models beat TF-IDF on the test split and under cross-validation, and the gap is roughly twice the fold-to-fold spread, so it is a real difference rather than noise. word2vec against fastText is a different story: 0.794 and 0.792 on the test split, 0.795 and 0.800 under cross-validation. They swap places depending on how you measure, which means this experiment cannot separate them.

Keep the size of the win in perspective. We went from 0.780 to about 0.795 — worth having, but not a transformation. Embeddings did not solve sentiment; they gave the same linear classifier better features. The reviews with no opinion in them are still wrong, because no feature can recover a signal that is not in the text.

What changed, review by review

164 fixed, 140 broken. The headline gain of 24 reviews out of 2 000 hides an enormous amount of churn: switching features changed the answer on 304 reviews and only came out 24 ahead. This is what a small accuracy improvement usually looks like from the inside, and it is why a single accuracy number is a poor way to decide that one model is “better”.

Two that fastText fixed, two that it broke
  • fixed, positive — ма шааллагь: not a Kazakh word at all, and TF-IDF had no column for it. fastText built a vector out of its letters.
  • fixed, positive — жаңа әндер мен өлеңдер жинағы: no sentiment word in sight; the embedding still placed it near the positive reviews.
  • broke, negative — кемшілігі көп: three words, and averaging three vectors blurs the one that carried the complaint.
  • broke, positive — өте керемет екен дартын дар: керемет was an unambiguous TF-IDF feature; in the average it is diluted by the noise around it.

The pattern in these four. Embeddings win where TF-IDF has no feature at all — misspellings, rare forms, reviews with no explicit sentiment word. They lose on short reviews built around one strong word, because averaging dilutes it. That is a description of when to use which, and it is more useful than the accuracy column.

Theory · The honest summary

What embeddings still cannot do

One vector per word, always

банк gets a single vector whether it is a financial institution or a river bank. Context cannot change it — the ambiguity from Lecture 0 is untouched.

Order is still lost

Averaging is a bag of words with better words. жақсы емес and its reverse give the same document vector.

Antonyms sit close together

Measured above: жақсы and нашар score 0.346, because they appear in the same contexts. For sentiment that is the worst possible confusion.

They inherit the corpus

Trained on app-store reviews, they know app-store Kazakh. Move to legal or medical text and the vectors are no longer yours to trust.

  • Bag of Words→
  • TF-IDF→
  • Static embeddings→
  • Contextual embeddings

The next step gives each occurrence of a word its own vector, computed from the sentence it sits in. That is what Transformers do, and it is the subject of the lectures after the next one.

In class

Knowledge check

Five questions to answer together before we finish.

Question 1

Embeddings are trained without any labels. Where does the training signal come from?

Show Answer

From the text itself: the model predicts which words appear near which. The corpus supplies both the question and the answer, which is why unlabelled Kazakh text is suddenly useful.

Question 2

word2vec skipped 2 156 tokens of the test set and fastText skipped none. Why?

Show Answer

word2vec only knows whole words it saw in training. fastText stores character n-grams, so it can assemble a vector for any string — including inflected forms it has never met.

Question 3

жақсы ~ нашар scored 0.346 — higher than an unrelated word. Is that a bug?

Show Answer

No. Antonyms appear in the same contexts, so the distributional hypothesis places them near each other by design. It is a known weakness, and it matters most for exactly our task — sentiment.

Question 4

Accuracy went from 0.780 to 0.794, but 140 reviews that were right became wrong. How do you report this?

Show Answer

Report both: a net gain of 24 reviews out of 2 000, with 304 answers changed. Compare the gain against the cross-validation spread before calling it an improvement, and say which reviews moved in each direction.

Question 5

We trained on 134 368 reviews but the classifier only uses 8 000. Why is that not a mistake?

Show Answer

They are two different jobs. Training vectors needs no labels, so every review is usable. Training a classifier needs labels and a balanced split, which is what limits it to 8 000. The embeddings carry what they learned from the other 126 000 into the classifier.

CBOW is faster and tends to be smoother on frequent words; skip-gram is usually better on rare ones, which is why it is the default here. Check the neighbours of a rare word in both.

Small windows pull towards words that fill the same grammatical slot; large windows pull towards the same topic. Expect теле2 and 4g to become more prominent as the window grows.

Set min_n=2, max_n=6 for fastText. Do the neighbours become more morphological or more typo-like?

Show Answer

Shorter n-grams match more strings, so typos get closer; longer ones demand more overlap. This is the dial between coverage and precision.

Weight each word vector by its idf inside MeanEmbedding. We measured 0.787 for word2vec — reproduce it and explain the drop.

idf plays down frequent words, and in sentiment the frequent words (жақсы, нашар, рахмет) are precisely the ones carrying the label.

The lists will differ. In a report, state the seed, the worker count and the library version, or run several seeds and report the spread — the same discipline as cross-validation.
