Theory · The problem

What 8 000 labelled reviews cannot buy

The newest architecture came last. We ruled out the obvious explanations by measurement: more epochs gave 0.749, a smaller model gave 0.750, a larger learning rate made it worse. Five times the data gave 0.787. The lever that worked was data, and only data.

The from-scratch model spends most of its 8 000 examples learning what Kazakh is, and only what is left over on the task we actually care about.

The observation that fixes it. Learning the language and learning the task are two different jobs. The first needs enormous amounts of text but no labels at all. The second needs labels but, if the first job is already done, not very many. So do them separately.

You have already seen this argument win once. Lecture 4 trained word2vec on all 134 368 unlabelled reviews and then handed the vectors to the Lecture 3 classifier, which improved. Lecture 5 initialised an LSTM’s embedding layer from those vectors and went from 0.772 to 0.787. Both are the same move as this lecture, applied to one layer. BERT applies it to the whole encoder.

Theory · The core idea

Pretrain once, fine-tune many times

Transfer learning splits training into two stages with completely different economics. Understanding that asymmetry is most of understanding why the field reorganised itself around it.

Pretraining is a public good: somebody spends weeks of GPU time, uploads the result, and every downstream task starts from it.

The two stages, side by side
what the data looks like
Pretraining: raw text, scraped, unlabelled, as much as you can find. Fine-tuning: your task’s labelled examples, however few you have
what the objective is
Pretraining: predict hidden words from their context. Fine-tuning: predict the label. The first is a proxy task nobody wants the answer to — it exists purely to force the model to learn the language
what it costs
Pretraining BERT-base in 2018 took 4 days on 16 TPUs. Fine-tuning it on our 8 000 reviews takes a few minutes on a laptop. The ratio is roughly four orders of magnitude
who does it
Pretraining: a research lab, once, and the weights are published. Fine-tuning: you, as often as you like
what changes in the weights
Pretraining: everything, from random. Fine-tuning: everything again, but gently — small steps from an already-good point rather than a search from nowhere

The name for the proxy task is self-supervision. The labels are manufactured from the text itself by hiding part of it, so an unlimited supply of supervised examples comes from data nobody had to annotate. That single trick is what makes modern NLP possible.

Theory · The pretraining task

Masked language modelling

To learn a language from raw text you need a question whose answer is already in the text. BERT’s question is: I have hidden some words — which words were they?

Masked language modelling. The label is the word that was removed, which makes every sentence in existence a free training example.

Why this particular task
it forces real understanding
To fill the gap in Бұл қосымша өте [MASK] екен the model must know what kind of word follows өте, that the sentence is about an app, and which adjectives Kazakh speakers use about apps. There is no shortcut
it can look both ways
This is the point of the name: Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. The word екен after the gap is as useful as the words before it, and BERT is allowed to use it
the labels are free
Nobody annotated anything. The corpus is the label, hidden from itself
it is not next-token prediction
A causal language model (Lecture 6, and Lecture 8) predicts the next token and may never look right. BERT gives up the ability to generate in exchange for seeing both sides — the trade-off from the previous lecture, made deliberately

Why 15%? Mask too little and each sentence teaches almost nothing per forward pass, so pretraining takes forever. Mask too much and too little context survives to make the gaps recoverable. 15% is an empirical compromise from the original paper; later work found the optimum is fairly flat and depends on model size.

The 80 / 10 / 10 detail

Of the tokens chosen for masking, BERT replaces only 80% with [MASK]. 10% are replaced by a random word, and 10% are left alone. This looks fussy and has a clear reason.

The model can never be sure whether the token it is looking at is right, so it has to form an opinion about every position rather than only the masked ones.

And the part that was dropped. The original BERT had a second objective, Next Sentence Prediction: given two segments, are they consecutive? It was meant to help question answering and inference. RoBERTa later removed it and scored better, and essentially nothing since has used it. This is a useful lesson about published recipes — parts of them are guesses, and only later measurement separates the load-bearing ideas from the decoration.

Theory · What BERT is

BERT is the encoder half of Lecture 6

There is no new architecture in this lecture. BERT is a stack of the encoder blocks you built last time, plus three input conventions and a pretraining objective.

Everything inside the dashed ENCODER box of Lecture 6’s full-architecture diagram, with [CLS] and [SEP] added and positions learned rather than sinusoidal.

The conventions worth knowing
[CLS]
A token prepended to every input whose only job is to accumulate a summary of the whole sequence. It has no meaning of its own; it is a place for the model to put an answer. Lecture 6 used mean pooling instead — both work, and [CLS] is the convention here because the pretrained weights were trained with it
[SEP]
A separator, so that two sentences can be fed as one input. This is what makes BERT usable for pair tasks: entailment, similarity, question answering over a passage
segment embeddings
A third embedding table with exactly two rows, marking “this token belongs to sentence A” or “to sentence B”
learned positions
Not the sine table of Lecture 6 but a plain embedding table indexed by position, with 512 rows. Simpler, and the reason BERT cannot read anything longer than 512 tokens
WordPiece
A sub-word vocabulary of 30 000 entries, or 119 547 for the multilingual version. The next section shows what it does to Kazakh

The two sizes from the paper: BERT-base is 12 blocks, 12 heads, d_model = 768, 110 M parameters — deliberately matched to GPT-1 so the comparison was fair. BERT-large is 24 blocks, 16 heads, d_model = 1024, 340 M parameters.

The tokenizer is part of the model

A pretrained model arrives with its own fixed vocabulary, and you cannot change it. For an agglutinative language that vocabulary decides a great deal.

Lecture 0 opened the course with the word кітаптарымыздан — “from our books” — as the example of Kazakh morphology. Here is what each pretrained tokenizer does with it.

The same word, three vocabularies. A tokenizer that fits the language turns one word into its actual morphemes; one that does not turns it into debris.

Why the split matters
fragments are harder to learn from
If кітап is a single token, every sentence containing any form of “book” contributes to the same embedding. If the root is split differently depending on the suffix, that evidence is scattered across unrelated pieces
more pieces means less context
BERT’s limit is 512 positions. A tokenizer that needs six pieces per word fits a third as much text as one that needs two — and attention cost grows with the square of the length
the vocabulary is frozen
You inherit whatever the pretraining team chose. This is the one part of a pretrained model you genuinely cannot fix by fine-tuning
bigger is not automatically better
kaz-RoBERTa has a 52 000-entry vocabulary against XLM-R’s 250 002 and still splits Kazakh better, because all 52 000 entries are spent on one language instead of a hundred

A practical rule: before choosing a pretrained model for a low-resource language, tokenize a few hundred words of your own data and count the pieces per word. It takes two minutes and predicts a surprising amount of what the fine-tuning results will look like.

Theory · The second stage

What fine-tuning actually does

Fine-tuning is a small idea with a few counter-intuitive details. The idea: throw away the pretraining head, bolt on a new one, and keep training — gently.

Two numbers worth holding together: 177 million weights arrive already trained, and 1 538 arrive random.

The details that are not obvious
everything trains, not just the head
The natural guess is to freeze the encoder and train only the new layer. It works, but not nearly as well — we measure the gap below. Letting the gradient reach all twelve blocks lets the representation itself adapt to the task
the learning rate is tiny
2e-5 here, against 3e-4 for the from-scratch model in Lecture 6 — about fifteen times smaller. You are starting from a good point and must not blow it away. Learning rates above about 1e-4 routinely destroy a pretrained model in the first few hundred steps
two to four epochs, not fifty
The original paper recommends 2–4 and that advice has held up. More epochs on a small dataset overfit quickly, because a 177 M-parameter model can memorise 8 000 short reviews without difficulty
warm-up and decay
The learning rate rises from zero over the first ~10% of steps and then decays linearly. The warm-up protects the pretrained weights while the random head is still producing nonsense gradients
the result is a separate model per task
Fine-tuning produces a new 177 M-parameter checkpoint. Three tasks means three copies. This is the cost that later methods — adapters, LoRA, prompting — exist to avoid, and Lecture 10 returns to it

Catastrophic forgetting is the thing to fear. Too large a learning rate, or too many epochs on too little data, and the model overwrites the general language knowledge that made it valuable. The symptom is training loss near zero and test accuracy worse than a linear model. The defences are all conservative: small steps, few epochs, warm-up. 

from transformers import AutoModelForSequenceClassification

for name in ("bert-base-multilingual-cased", "xlm-roberta-base"):

Theory · The family

BERT’s descendants, and what each one changed

BERT was published in 2018 and immediately became a platform. Everything below keeps the encoder stack and changes one thing: the data, the objective, the parameter budget, or the size.

RoBERTa — the same model, trained properly

What changed

Ten times the data (160 GB against 16 GB), much longer training, larger batches, dynamic masking — a fresh mask every epoch instead of one fixed at preprocessing — byte-level BPE with 50 000 entries, and no Next Sentence Prediction.

What it showed

The architecture was never the bottleneck: BERT had simply been undertrained. RoBERTa-base beats BERT-large on several GLUE tasks with fewer parameters. “Robustly optimised” is the whole contribution, and it was a large one.

DistilBERT and TinyBERT — the same knowledge, smaller

A 110 M-parameter model is awkward to serve: it is slow on CPU, too big for a phone, and expensive at scale. Distillation trains a small student to imitate a large teacher.

Knowledge distillation. The teacher’s soft probabilities carry more information per example than the correct answer alone, which is why the student learns so efficiently.

The two distilled models by name
DistilBERT
Six blocks instead of twelve, each initialised from every second block of the teacher. 66 M parameters, about 40% smaller and 60% faster, and it keeps roughly 97% of BERT’s GLUE score. Trained with three losses at once: ordinary MLM, the distillation loss against the teacher’s distribution, and a cosine loss pulling the student’s hidden states towards the teacher’s
TinyBERT
Goes considerably further. It distils not only the output distribution but the attention matrices and hidden states of intermediate layers, and it does so twice: once on general text, then again on the specific task with augmented data. The 4-layer, 312-dimension version is 14.5 M parameters — about 7.5× smaller and 9.4× faster than BERT-base — while retaining around 96% of its GLUE score
MiniLM
Distils the attention distributions themselves, self-attention value-relations included, and is the approach behind the small multilingual models in our comparison table

The honest caveat on all distillation numbers: “97% of the performance” is an average over GLUE, which is English and mostly easy. On a harder or lower-resource task the gap is usually wider. We measure our own below rather than quoting theirs.

Three of those deserve a sentence more
ALBERT — small in storage, not in time
It shares one set of block weights across all twelve layers and splits the embedding matrix into two smaller ones. 12 M parameters against 110 M sounds dramatic, and the file really is that small — but it still runs twelve blocks at inference, so it is not faster. It is the one model in this table whose headline number is routinely misread
ELECTRA — a better use of every example
Instead of masking, a small generator substitutes plausible words and the main model decides, for every token, whether it was replaced. BERT learns from 15% of positions; ELECTRA learns from 100%. ELECTRA-small reaches GPT-level GLUE scores with roughly 1/30 of the compute, which makes it the strongest choice when the pretraining budget is small
DeBERTa — content and position, separately
Each token carries two vectors, one for what it is and one for where it is, and attention is computed from content-to-content and content-to-position terms separately rather than from one summed vector. It was the first model to pass the human baseline on SuperGLUE, and DeBERTa-v3 — which adds ELECTRA-style pretraining — is still a very strong encoder today

Where the specialised models fit. Beyond the general family there are domain models (BioBERT, SciBERT, FinBERT) and language models (RuBERT, and for us kaz-roberta-conversational), all built by continuing pretraining on text from one field or one language. When your data looks like theirs, they usually win.

import torch
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader, TensorDataset
from transformers import (AutoModelForSequenceClassification, AutoTokenizer,
                          get_linear_schedule_with_warmup)

name = "xlm-roberta-base"
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(name, num_labels=2).to(device)

# 1. the tokenizer that came with the model turns text into ids and a mask
batch = tokenizer(list(texts), truncation=True, max_length=64,
                  padding="max_length", return_tensors="pt")
data = TensorDataset(batch["input_ids"], batch["attention_mask"], labels)
batches = DataLoader(data, batch_size=32, shuffle=True)

# 2. a small learning rate, warm-up, and only three passes over the data
optimiser = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=2e-5, weight_decay=0.01)
steps = len(batches) * 3
schedule = get_linear_schedule_with_warmup(optimiser, int(0.1 * steps), steps)

# 3. an ordinary training loop — nothing about it is specific to BERT
for epoch in range(3):
    model.train()
    for ids, mask, labels in batches:
        optimiser.zero_grad()
        loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(
            model(input_ids=ids, attention_mask=mask).logits, labels)
        loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        optimiser.step()
        schedule.step()
What is different from Lecture 6’s loop
three lines instead of a model class
Lecture 6 wrote MultiHeadAttention, Block and Transformer by hand. Here from_pretrained replaces all of it — and it is worth remembering, while typing that one line, that it is loading exactly the architecture you built
the tokenizer is not ours
No build_vocab, no min_count. The vocabulary ships with the weights and must not be changed
an attention mask, not a padding mask
Same idea, opposite polarity: attention_mask is 1 for real tokens, where our pad_mask was True for padding. A very common source of silently inverted masks
a learning-rate schedule
Lecture 6 used a constant rate. Fine-tuning is sensitive enough that warm-up and decay are worth the two extra lines

The loop itself is unchanged. Forward, loss, backward, clip, step — the same five operations as Lecture 5’s LSTM. Nothing about fine-tuning a 278 M-parameter model requires new machinery.

The headline: 0.750 → 0.799. The same architecture family, the same 8 000 labels, the same test set. The only change is that the encoder started from weights that had already read a lot of text. That is worth +0.049 — and it converts the worst model in the course into the best one.

Now the uncomfortable part

Look at the spread within Lecture 7. Best 0.799, worst 0.789: a range of 0.010 across models that differ by 3.3× in size, by a factor of three in speed, and enormously in how well they handled Kazakh in the two diagnostics above.

One big step and four small ones. The choice between pretrained encoders is worth about a fifth of the choice to use one at all.

A 0.010 spread on a 2 000-row test set is not a ranking. Twenty reviews decide it, and re-running with a different seed moves a fine-tuned model by roughly that much. The honest statement is that these five models are tied on this task, and any claim that mBERT “beat” XLM-R here would not survive a second seed.

What the diagnostics did predict

Why the five-class task refuses to move
the ceiling is in the labels, not the model
Lectures 5 and 6 both printed the confusion matrix, and both showed the same shape: the ends of the scale are found, the middle is not, and most errors are off by exactly one star. A 4-star and a 5-star review are often the same sentence written by two different people. No encoder can recover a distinction the data does not contain
every model converges to the same place
0.358 to 0.381 across five very different models, and 0.374 to 0.378 for the two from-scratch models of Lectures 5 and 6. Seven models across two training regimes, all landing in the same narrow band, is what an intrinsic ceiling looks like
1 600 examples per class, not 4 000
Holding the training set at 8 000 rows means 1 600 examples per class instead of the 4 000 that polarity got. Some of the flatness is simply that
but the spread more than doubled
0.023 here against 0.010 on polarity. On the harder task the models stop being interchangeable, and MiniLM — the one that was tied on polarity — falls clearly to last

This is the prediction from the previous section, confirmed. We said the diagnostics would matter more on a harder task or with fewer labels. Both conditions hold here, and the model that the tokenizer and fill-in-the-blank tests liked best — kaz-RoBERTa — now finishes first outright, in a third of XLM-R’s time and a third of its size.

The honest summary of two results. Pretraining is not a uniform multiplier. On a task with real signal that a model has to learn the language to extract, it is transformative. On a task whose difficulty is irreducible ambiguity in the labels, it moves almost nothing — and a careful practitioner reports the second result as loudly as the first, because the alternative is promising a stakeholder an improvement that the data cannot deliver.

And now the comparison that matters

Not a statement about the languages. A statement about how much of each one was in the pretraining corpora.

0.937 against 0.799. Same recipe, same 8 000 rows, same task, sentences of the same length. The English model gets 87% of the way from guessing to perfect; the Kazakh model gets 60%. Expressed as errors, which is the more honest framing, the Kazakh system makes 3.2× as many mistakes.
What that gap is and is not
it is not about the languages
Nothing about Kazakh makes sentiment intrinsically harder to detect than in English. Both tasks are “did this person like the thing”
it is about the pretraining corpus
RoBERTa read 160 GB of English. XLM-R read 2.5 TB across 100 languages, of which Kazakh is a small share; mBERT read Wikipedia, where Kazakh is smaller still. The diagnostics at the start of this lecture showed the consequence directly — mBERT could not produce a Kazakh verb
it is partly about the tokenizer
English words are mostly one token. Kazakh words cost two to six, so the same 64-token budget holds far less meaning, and the evidence for each root is scattered
it is the argument for language-specific models
kaz-RoBERTa — 83 M parameters trained on Kazakh alone — was first on the harder Kazakh task and fastest on both. That is where the gap gets closed

The practical lesson for anyone building NLP for Kazakhstan: the published numbers you read in papers are English numbers. Budget for the gap, measure on your own language, and treat a Kazakh-specific pretrained model as a first-class option rather than a curiosity.

Practice · The decision

How to choose a model, in practice

“Take the biggest one” is wrong often enough to be worth replacing with a short procedure.

Language first, then a measured comparison, then compression only if a deployment constraint demands it.

The questions to ask in order
1. what does the tokenizer do to my text?
Tokenize a few hundred of your own sentences and count pieces per word. Two minutes, no training, and it eliminates the obviously wrong candidates before you spend any GPU time
2. is there a model for my language or domain?
If there is, it is a strong candidate, but not automatically the winner — a small specialised model may have been trained on far less text than XLM-R was. Measure both rather than assuming
3. what is my latency and memory budget?
Ask before training, not after. If the answer is “CPU, 50 ms, on a phone”, the large models were never candidates and the comparison should have been between distilled ones from the start
4. how many labels do I have?
With a few hundred, the pretrained model is doing nearly all the work and the choice of model matters more than anything else you can control. With hundreds of thousands, the gaps narrow and a from-scratch model becomes viable again
5. only then: which is most accurate?
Run the same recipe over three or four candidates. It is an afternoon of laptop time and it replaces an argument with a table

And report the spread, not the best run. Fine-tuning is noticeably seed-sensitive on small datasets; differences below about 0.01 on a 2 000-row test set are not differences. Three seeds and a range is the honest way to report a comparison like this one.

Theory · The honest summary

What this still does not give you

One model per task

Every fine-tune produces a full copy of the weights. Ten tasks means ten checkpoints and ten deployments. Adapters and LoRA exist to fix exactly this, and Lecture 10 covers them.

It still needs labels

Fewer than before — we measure how many below — but not zero. A model that can be told what to do in words, with no labels at all, is the subject of Lecture 9.

It cannot generate

An encoder produces representations, not text. Ask BERT to write a review and there is no mechanism for it to do so. That is Lecture 8.

512 tokens, and quadratic

The learned position table has 512 rows and attention cost grows with the square of the length. Long documents need either truncation, chunking, or a different architecture.

The tokenizer is frozen

We measured what mBERT does to Kazakh morphology. Fine-tuning cannot repair it; only a different pretrained model can.

It inherits what it read

Biases, factual errors and coverage gaps in the pretraining corpus arrive with the weights. mBERT believing Almaty is the capital is a harmless example of a general problem.

  • TF-IDF→
  • word2vec→
  • LSTM→
  • Transformer→
  • pretrained encoder→
  • generative LLM

The course has now made the same move twice: Lecture 4 pretrained the embedding layer on unlabelled text, and Lecture 7 pretrained the whole encoder. The next step keeps the pretraining and drops the encoder’s one restriction — the inability to produce text. That is a decoder, trained to predict the next token, at a scale where fine-tuning starts to become optional.

What the script does, in six steps
1. data
balance() is copied unchanged from Lecture 3 so the split is bit-for-bit the one every previous lecture used. KazSAnDRA is read from the local cache if present, otherwise downloaded
2. tokenizer
AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained — the vocabulary belongs to the model, so this line changes when the model changes
3. model
AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(name, num_labels=k) downloads the encoder and attaches a fresh head of the right width
4. train

5. compare

6. inspect

Hardware. These runs were done on an Apple M4 Pro using the MPS backend — a laptop, on purpose. On CPU alone expect roughly ten times longer; on a recent NVIDIA GPU, roughly five times shorter. None of it requires a cluster, which is exactly the point of the pretrain-once-fine-tune-many split.

In class

Knowledge check

Eight questions we answer together before finishing.

Question 1

Why can pretraining use billions of words when our labelled dataset has only 8 000 rows?

Show Answer

Because the pretraining labels are manufactured from the text itself by hiding words. No human annotated anything, so any text at all becomes supervised data. That is what “self-supervised” means, and it is the entire reason the two-stage recipe is possible.

Question 2

Why does BERT use masked language modelling instead of next-token prediction?

Show Answer

Because it wants to be bidirectional. Predicting the next token forces a causal mask, so the model may never look right. Masking a word in the middle lets every other position, on both sides, contribute — which builds a better representation but gives up the ability to generate.

Question 3

During fine-tuning, what is new and what is reused? Give rough parameter counts.

Show Answer

For mBERT: 177 853 440 pretrained encoder weights are reused, and a Linear(768, 2) head of 1 538 weights is new and random. The pretraining head that predicted words over the vocabulary is discarded. Everything then trains together, gently.

Question 4

Why is the fine-tuning learning rate about fifteen times smaller than Lecture 6’s?

Show Answer

Because you are starting from a good point rather than searching from nowhere. Large steps overwrite the pretrained knowledge in the first few hundred updates — catastrophic forgetting — and the model ends up worse than one trained from scratch. Practice task 3 makes this happen on purpose.

Question 5

RoBERTa kept BERT’s architecture unchanged. What did it change, and what did that prove?

Show Answer

Ten times the data, much longer training, dynamic masking, larger batches, and no Next Sentence Prediction. It proved BERT had been undertrained rather than badly designed — and, by dropping NSP and improving, that part of the original recipe was decoration.

Question 6

DistilBERT trains on the teacher’s full probability distribution rather than the correct answer. Why does that help?

Show Answer

Because the distribution carries more information per example. “пайдалы 0.41, жақсы 0.19, керемет 0.08” tells the student which other answers were also reasonable and how close they were — a similarity structure that a one-hot label throws away entirely.

Question 7

Our five pretrained models scored between 0.789 and 0.799. What is the correct way to report that?

Show Answer

As a tie. A 0.010 spread on a 2 000-row test set is twenty reviews, which is within the seed-to-seed variation of fine-tuning. The defensible claim is “all five are equivalent on this task”, and the interesting differences are in size and speed, not accuracy.

Question 8

mBERT has the worst Kazakh tokenizer and the worst fill-in-the-blank predictions, yet finished joint best. Explain.

Show Answer

The diagnostics predict how fast a model reaches its ceiling, not where the ceiling is. After one epoch the ranking matched them exactly — kaz-RoBERTa 0.800, mBERT 0.762. Given three epochs of 8 000 labelled examples on a fairly easy binary task, every model catches up. With fewer labels, or a harder task, the gap would have persisted.

Summary

Today we learned

  • Learning a language and learning a task are separate jobs with separate data needs
  • Masked language modelling manufactures labels from raw text, so pretraining needs no annotation
  • BERT is Lecture 6’s encoder stack plus [CLS], [SEP], segment embeddings and learned positions
  • 15% masking, split 80 / 10 / 10, and why NSP was later dropped
  • Fine-tuning discards the pretraining head, adds a tiny new one, and trains everything gently
  • A learning rate fifteen times smaller, three epochs, warm-up — all to avoid catastrophic forgetting
  • RoBERTa: same architecture, more data, proving BERT was undertrained
  • DistilBERT and TinyBERT: a student copies the teacher’s whole distribution, not just its answer
  • ALBERT shares weights, ELECTRA learns from every token, DeBERTa splits content from position
  • The tokenizer ships with the weights and cannot be fixed by fine-tuning
  • What a pretrained model knows before fine-tuning, measured two ways
  • Pretraining is worth far more than the choice of which pretrained model to use
  • A spread smaller than your seed variance is a tie, not a ranking
